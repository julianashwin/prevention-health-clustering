	\subsection{Setup: deterministic type and stochastic noise}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Health of person $i$ of latent type $j$ at age $a$ (age counted from a base age, not birth):
		\begin{equation}
			h_{i,a} = \bar h_{j,a} + u_{i,a}, \qquad \bar h_{j,a} \equiv H_j + \sum_{a'=1}^{a} d_{j,a'} .
			\label{eq:levels}
		\end{equation}
		$(H_j, d_{j,a})$ is the \emph{deterministic} component: the type's level at the base age and its expected change at each age. $u_{i,a}$ is the \emph{stochastic} component, mean zero within type, otherwise unrestricted (could be transitory, persistent, or measurement error).
		\item Three kinds of information in a health history: 
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item what \emph{stays} (the level, including permanent shocks that have landed),
			\item what is \emph{in the moment} (transitory variation and measurement error), 
			\item how one is \emph{evolving} ($d_{j,a}$, the only component that predicts future \emph{changes}).
		\end{enumerate} 
		Prevention targets the third; the first two are what is paid for, as treatment, once realised.
		\item Only what is carried from one wave to the next produces covariance between waves: the three components leave different traces on the covariance matrix, which is the basis for our identification.
		\item Two-by-two matrix of possibilities: dispersion is deterministic (across types) or stochastic (within type), and actionable or not. Deterministic and actionable is \emph{targetable} prevention; stochastic and actionable is untargeted prevention, or treatment once the shock has arrived. Predictability of levels $\neq$ predictability of changes.
		\item Health types are thus the object of interest: they are the support of $(H, d)$, and clustering estimates it. Between/within variance maps to type/individual. Result~\ref{res:prev} below is the reason: the return to prevention for a type is a function of that type's mean path alone, so a policy needs the type shares and each type's path, which is what Section~\ref{sec:types} estimates.
		\item Link to other life course: With the base age at birth, the life-course accounts are second moments: critical period $=\Var(H)$, accumulation $=\Var(d)$, pathway $=\Cov(H,d)$; at a later base age the triple's evolution with the base age separates them.
		\item Throughout this section, we will use a linear illustration (constant type slopes, $d_{j,a}=d_j$): a random-coefficients growth model with $V_H, V_d, C_{Hd}$ and noise variances $v_a$,
		\begin{equation}
			\begin{gathered}
				\bar h_a=\bar H+a\bar d, 
				\\ 
				\Var(h_a)=V_H+2aC_{Hd}+a^2V_d+v_a, 
				\\
				\Cov(h_s,h_t)=V_H+(s+t)C_{Hd}+st\,V_d+\Cov(u_s,u_t).
			\end{gathered}
			\label{eq:linear}
		\end{equation}
		\item A key issue that will come up again in relation to costs and when we bring to the data is what the units of health are. The covariance matrix treats health as cardinal, so transformations of a health index that are rank neutral can change the covariance matrix. We report the unbounded latent scale $\theta$ in the main text; its bounded expected-score twin $h$ and two frailty indices are in the appendix, and these are all discussed in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:data}.
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\subsection{Policy: the treatment bill, and prevention as avoiding it}
	\label{sec:policy_fw}
	
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Cost of poor health $c(h)=(1-h)^2$, full health normalised to one. The quadratic is for illustration: it is the second-order approximation to any smooth increasing convex cost, and it makes the first two moments of health sufficient for every policy object below. What matters for prevention is not the quadratic itself but marginal cost times the decline prevention holds back (\eqref{eq:Pj_general} below). The quadratic is also not far from the data: in pounds, cost is close to quadratic in poor health (Section~\ref{sec:data}, Figure~\ref{fig:cost}), with a cubic term that only steepens the sickest end. The prevention exercises in Section~\ref{sec:policy} price with the cost curve estimated in pounds on $\theta$ rather than the quadratic.
		\item Population cost at age $a$: mean gap plus between-type plus within-type variance,
		\begin{equation}
			\E[c(h_{i,a})]=(1-\bar h_a)^2+\Var_j(\bar h_{j,a})+\textstyle\sum_j\pi_j\Var(u_{i,a}\mid j).
			\label{eq:popcost}
		\end{equation}
		\item Cost is treatment: $c(h)$ is what the health system spends on a person in health $h$, and the cost index of Section~\ref{sec:data} measures exactly that (utilisation at unit costs). The object a policy acts on is the \textbf{treatment bill still to come} from age $r$,
		\begin{equation}
			C(r)=\sum_{a\ge r}\E[c(h_{i,a})]=\sum_{a\ge r}\Bigl[\underbrace{c(\bar h_a)}_{\text{mean path}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_j\pi_{j,a}c(\bar h_{j,a})-c(\bar h_a)}_{\text{type differences}}+\underbrace{\E[c(h_{i,a})]-\textstyle\sum_j\pi_{j,a}c(\bar h_{j,a})}_{\text{realised deviations}}\Bigr],
			\label{eq:C}
		\end{equation}
		which for the quadratic is \eqref{eq:popcost} summed over ages: the mean gap, the between-type variance and the within-type variance. The third part is paid as treatment but is not preventable: it is the in-the-moment variation, including the ``spike''.
		\item One instrument. \textbf{Prevention} is ex ante and acts on change: from age $r$ at intensity $\mu$ it scales every future mean change of a type by $(1-\mu)$, moving type $j$'s path to
		$$
		\bar h^{\mu}_{j,a}=\bar h_{j,a}+\mu\,(\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})\quad\text{for } a\ge r,
		$$
		and leaving $u_{i,a}$ untouched, so health gained is $\mu$ times the type's preventable decline $\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}$, and its return is the treatment cost that avoids.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\begin{result}[The treatment bill; note Result 1]\label{res:treat}
		$$
		C(r)=\sum_{a\ge r}\bigl[(1-\bar h_a)^2+\Var(h_{i,a})\bigr]
		$$
		under the quadratic. Cross-sectional moments only; no identification problem. (The note's ``return to treatment'' $T(r)$ is $2C(r)$: restoring a share $\tau$ of every realised gap is worth $\tau$ times twice the bill.)
	\end{result}
	
	\begin{result}[Return to prevention by type; note Results 2 and 3]\label{res:prev}
		Under the quadratic, the return to prevention from age $r$ for type $j$, per unit of $\mu$, is
		\begin{equation}
			P_j(r)=2\sum_{a\ge r}\underbrace{(1-\bar h_{j,a})}_{\text{gap at }a}\;\underbrace{(\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})}_{\text{preventable decline by }a}\;\equiv\;\sum_{a\ge r}s_j(a\mid r).
			\label{eq:Pj}
		\end{equation}
		Both factors are functions of the type's mean path alone: nothing within type enters. A type pays back only where it is \emph{both} in poor health and still declining. A sick but flat type is a treatment case; a declining but healthy type pays back only once its decline has opened a gap, which is why the per-age saving $s_j(a\mid r)$ rises with age.
	\end{result}

	\begin{corollary}[The uniform policy]\label{cor:uniform}
		Giving every type the same $\mu$ returns $P(r)=\sum_j\pi_jP_j(r)$, and the law of total covariance applied to the product in \eqref{eq:Pj} splits it as
		\begin{equation}
			P(r)=2\sum_{a\ge r}\Bigl[\underbrace{(1-\bar h_a)(\bar h_{r-1}-\bar h_a)}_{\text{mean channel}}
			+\underbrace{\Cov_j\bigl(1-\bar h_{j,a},\,\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}\bigr)}_{\text{between-type channel}}\Bigr],
			\label{eq:P}
		\end{equation}
		the between-type channel splitting further as $\Cov_j(1-H_j,\,\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})+\Cov_j(H_j-\bar h_{j,r-1},\,\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})+\Var_j(\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})$, the pathway, persistence and accumulation margins. In the data the mean channel is most of the uniform return (three-fifths from 25, nine-tenths from 70; Section~\ref{sec:policy}), so the cross-section prices uniform prevention nearly on its own; what the types add is the dispersion of $P_j(r)$ across them, which is what targeting buys (Result~\ref{res:target}).
	\end{corollary}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{What matters is marginal cost times decline.} For a general increasing convex cost, to first order in $\mu$,
		\begin{equation}
			P_j(r)=\sum_{a\ge r}G_{j,a}\,(\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}),\qquad G_{j,a}\equiv\E[-c'(h_{i,a})\mid j],
			\label{eq:Pj_general}
		\end{equation}
		the marginal cost of a unit of health to type $j$ at age $a$ times the decline prevention holds back by then. The quadratic's gap is this marginal cost, since $-c'(h)=2(1-h)$. The uniform return splits as in Corollary~\ref{cor:uniform} with $G$ in place of the gap: $P(r)=\sum_{a\ge r}\bigl[\bar G_a\,\bar d_a+\Cov_j(G_{j,a},d_{j,a})\bigr]$, with $d_{j,a}=\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}$. Two features are specific to the quadratic: within-type dispersion drops out exactly (for a cubic it enters exactly, $G_{j,a}=-c'(\E[h_{i,a}\mid j])-\tfrac12c'''\Var(h_{i,a}\mid j)$, and on the estimated curve the dispersion term is 8--10\% of the uniform return), and the between-type channel splits into the pathway, persistence and accumulation margins.
		\item \textbf{Why the evolution of the mean by type.} Had prevention instead lifted the path by a constant $\delta$ from $r$, the return would be $2\delta\sum_{a\ge r}(1-\bar h_{j,a})$: gap only, priced by levels, and a cross-section would do. The product form of \eqref{eq:Pj} is specific to prevention that acts on the \emph{change}, and under it the object a policy needs is $(\pi_j,\bar h_{j,\cdot})$, the support of the types and each type's path, not the covariance structure. The covariance structure matters for two other things: separating the deterministic path from the noise (Section~\ref{sec:ident_fw}), and recovering a person's type when it is not observed (the posterior, Section~\ref{sec:policy}).
		\item For a general increasing convex cost $c(\cdot)$ the bill is \eqref{eq:C} as written and the return keeps the form in Result~\ref{res:prev} with the derivative as the weight; the quadratic reproduces Result~\ref{res:treat} and \eqref{eq:Pj}. $P(r)/C(r)$ sets the return against the remaining bill. $P(r)$ is a first-order return, the saving per unit of $\mu$ as $\mu$ rises from zero, so on a convex curve it overstates what a larger dose saves and $P(r)/C(r)$ can exceed one. For a cubic cost, the curve Section~\ref{sec:policy} estimates, the expansion stops at the third term and the saving at any $\mu$ is exact: with $d_{j,a}=\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}$,
		\begin{equation}
			\begin{aligned}
				S_j(r;\mu)&\equiv\sum_{a\ge r}\E\bigl[c(h_{i,a})-c(h_{i,a}+\mu d_{j,a})\bigm| j\bigr]\\
				&=\mu P_j(r)-\frac{\mu^2}{2}\sum_{a\ge r}c''\bigl(\E[h_{i,a}\mid j]\bigr)\,d_{j,a}^2-\frac{\mu^3}{6}\,c'''\sum_{a\ge r}d_{j,a}^3,
			\end{aligned}
			\label{eq:Sexact}
		\end{equation}
		since $c''$ is linear and $c'''$ a constant. Section~\ref{sec:policy} reports $S(r)\equiv\sum_j\pi_jS_j(r;1)$, the saving from holding back the whole decline, beside $P(r)$; unlike $P(r)$, it cannot exceed the bill.
		\item Prevention averts future gaps but must be assigned ex ante; the uniform policy pays the population-average preventable decline and its between-type channel is bounded by deterministic heterogeneity. What it can reach at most is the first two parts of \eqref{eq:C}: the realised deviations are treated, not prevented.
		\item Measurement error sits in the third part of the bill and in no prevention return; Case~4 below removes it from the type dynamics.
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\begin{result}[Targeting; note Results 5--7 and Lemma C.1]\label{res:target}~\\
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Any allocation $\mu(z)$ with the same average intensity saves $\Cov(\mu(z),\E[P_i\mid z])$ over the uniform policy, at most $\mathrm{sd}(\mu)\,\mathrm{sd}(\E[P_i\mid z])$: a signal is worth the return dispersion it reveals. 
			\item We can consider two forms of targetting:
			\item Targeting on initial health $h_{i,0}$ has a gap channel and a compounding channel, both cells of the first row of the covariance matrix, attenuated by the level/noise share $\lambda_0$.
			\item Targeting on type $\max_jP_j(r)-\sum_j\pi_jP_j(r)$.
		\end{itemize} 
	\end{result}
	
	\subsection{Identification}
	\label{sec:ident_fw}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Every observed covariance is a deterministic (i.e. between-type) part plus a noise part, $\Cov(h_s,h_t)=\Cov_j(\bar h_{j,s},\bar h_{j,t})+\Cov(u_{i,s},u_{i,t})$. The returns need the deterministic part but we only observe the sum. 
		\item Start with some broad assumptions: 
		\begin{itemize}
			\item A1 window linearity ($d_{j,a}=d_j$ over the waves used), 
			\item A2 orthogonality of the noise to $(H,d)$, 
			\item A3 a noise class that puts some restrictions on the covariance matrix
		\end{itemize}
		\item In the linear case
		\begin{equation}
			\Cov(h_s,h_t)=V_H+(s+t)C_{Hd}+st\,V_d+\Cov(u_s,u_t).
			\label{eq:forward}
		\end{equation}
		which means that on the diagonal $\Var(h_a)=V_H+2aC_{Hd}+a^2V_d+v_a$
		\item If the noise variances $v_a$ are unrestricted the variance profile can never rule out pure noise. 
		\item With stationary noise, $\Var(h_a)$ can identify $(C_{Hd},V_d)$ but never split $V_H$ from $\sigma^2_u$. Identification thus needs restrictions on how the noise variance moves with age and at least some off-diagonals.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\note{I am not super happy with ``spike" here (Claude came up with is) but I am also not really happy with ``measurement error" either as the shock could also be a genuine transitory health shock...}
	
	\begin{proposition}[Identification within the AR-plus-``spike" class; note Proposition 1]\label{prop:main} ~\\
		Assume A1--A2 and $\Cov(u_s,u_t)=\rho^{\,t-s}v_s+\mathbf 1\{s=t\}\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,s}$ for $s\le t$, $\rho\in[0,1)$: a carried component with memory $\rho$ and variances $v_s$, plus a one-period spike. Then  \\
		(i) five waves identify $V_H,C_{Hd},V_d,\rho$ and both noise variances at the waves used; \\
		(ii) four waves are sufficient without the spike (Case~3) or with both noise variances stationary (Case~4); \\
		(iii) three waves are sufficient without the spike absent and stationary variance, for any $\rho$ (Cases~1--2); \\
		(iv) two waves are sufficient if in addition $C_{Hd}=0$ and the noise is memoryless.
	\end{proposition}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The forces underlying , one per region of the matrix. This is the precise content of I(i) and I(ii):
		\begin{itemize}
			\item[I(i)] \textbf{Diagonal} $V(a)$: every component adds variance. Stationary noise cancels in differences and leaves $C_{Hd}$ (slope) and $V_d$ (curvature). Fails when $v_a$ drifts.
			\item[I(ii)] \textbf{First row} $C_0(a)$: the deterministic part is the line $V_H+aC_{Hd}$, carried noise a decay $\rho^av_0$; a line cannot mimic a decay, which splits the level from the noise and measures the memory; the row's slope beyond the noise is $C_{Hd}$ itself, locally $C_{Hd}(s)$ for the row anchored at $s$. A one-period spike is invisible to the row and is read as the anchor's excess over the row's back-extrapolation. Limit: $\rho\to1$ merges permanent shocks with the level.
			\item[I(iii)] \textbf{Interior}: $st\,V_d$ needs both indices to move; delivers $V_d$ when the diagonal cannot be read, overidentifying tests otherwise.
		\end{itemize}
		\item \note{There are a whole bunch of cases in the conceptual framework note, but we certainly don't want to go through all of them here, so we should think about what we want to include here. At least one or two are probably useful to help illustrate the identification (but maybe they can also wait until Section \ref{sec:data}... In the illustrative cases on real data, the sign of the small $C_{Hd}$ does depends on the noise treatment (although the gradient from positive to negative is relatively consistent.}
	\end{itemize}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The linearity assumption is local, so A1 holds only over a window. But given this local linearity, $C_{Hd}(a)\equiv\Cov_j(\bar h_{j,a},d_j)$ and so the deterministic variance increases by $C_{Hd}(a)+C_{Hd}(a+1)$ per year: a variance profile that flattens requires $C_{Hd}(a)$ to change sign, which the global linear model cannot produce. Figure~\ref{fig:windows} is the empirical counterpart: types refitted on age windows recover the lifecycle classes once the window reaches back to 40, and lose the declining type when it does not, because a slope needs the years over which it acts. 
		\item The between-type channel of $P(r)$ is the exposure-weighted sum $\sum_{a\ge r}(a-r+1)C_{Hd}(a)$ and so depends crucially on $C_{Hd}$.
		\item Everything can be represented as person-level OLS of $h$ on $(1,a)$, except for the noise correction which is case-specific (note Appendix E).
	\end{itemize}

	\paragraph{What the covariance surface prices without types.}
	In the linear case of Equation~\ref{eq:linear}, $\bar h_{j,a}=H_j+a\,d_j$, the between-type channel of the uniform return (Corollary~\ref{cor:uniform}) is $(a-r+1)(C_{Hd}+aV_d)$, so
	\begin{equation}
		P(r)=2\sum_{a\ge r}\bigl[(1-\bar h_a)(\bar h_{r-1}-\bar h_a)+ (a-r+1)\,C_{Hd} + a(a-r+1)\,V_d\bigr].
		\label{eq:Pr}
	\end{equation}
	The uniform return is therefore priced by the surface's moments alone, without recovering the types, and each object reads off one region of the surface:

The between-type channel this prices is also the moments-only check on the clustering: it should match the channel the estimated paths of Section~\ref{sec:types} imply.

	\subsection{Data and the health measure}
	
	
	
	Data:
	\begin{itemize}
			\item Understanding Society (UKHLS), waves 1--15, 2009--2024 \citep{ukhls}; adults aged 20--90. The K-means types and the Bayesian mixtures are fitted on exactly these rows.}
	\end{itemize}

	\note{Maybe here (or back in the introduction) we can already discuss that there is no single cardinal health measure and so, arguably, any monotonic transformation of a health measure is a valid health measure [see Cunha and Heckman (2008) on test scores; Cunha et al (2021) on ``Cardinality"; Bond and Lang (2013, 2019) for test scores and happiness scales]. However, if we want to use the covariance matrix to identify dynamics, then the scale does matter as these covariance are cardinal. So what we would need is some cardinal anchor and fortunately our framework gives us two obvious candidates: the mapping to costs and the mapping to interventions. Given that the focus of this paper is the potential returns to prevention rather than an evaluation of specific interventions it probably makes sense to focus more on the costs (and we can construct a proxy measure for these from the data on utilisation). If we had constructed the framework around mortality or QALYs or employment/productivity then this would imply an alternative anchoring.}

	To construct a continuous health metric from these underlying variables we use a graded-response model \cite{samejima1969} - a one-factor model for ordinal indicators (with continuous and unbounded indicators this would be equivalent to the linear factor models common in economics, e.g.\ \citealp{cunha2010,attanasio2020,agostinelli2025}). Let $Y_j \in {1,...,k_j}$ be the response to item $j$, which has $k_j$ ordered categories. Given the respondent's underlying health state is $\theta$, the probability of answering at or above category $c$ is logistic in $\theta$,
	\begin{equation}
			P(Y_j \ge c \mid \theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \exp\{-a_j(\theta - b_{j,c-1})\}}
	\end{equation}
	where $a_j$ and the $k_j-1$ thresholds $b_{j1} < \dots < b_{j,k_j-1}$ are estimated item-specific parameters. The latent variable $\theta$ thus capture the respondent's underlying health that determines their expected answers across all items. The item parameters are estimated by marginal maximum likelihood. Latent health is assumed standard normal in the pooled sample, which fixes the scale; $\theta$ is integrated out of the likelihood of each answer pattern over a grid of 121 points, and the discriminations and thresholds are chosen to maximise the resulting likelihood by EM, over the 28,360 distinct answer patterns. Scoring is a second step with the item parameters held fixed: each person-wave's posterior over $\theta$ is the standard normal prior times the likelihood of its eight answers, and the reported $\theta$ is the mean of that posterior, with its standard deviation kept alongside. Each person-wave is scored on its own, with the same prior, so a respondent's other waves and age do not inform their score.
	
	\note{we never take the posterior uncertainty around $\theta$ into the clustering models, which it might be useful to do (and I think relatively straightforward).} 
	
	The model allows thresholds the be unevenly spaced and for different items to separate observations in different parts of the distribution. In our case PF, RP and LIM\_FL discriminate the most (i.e. have the highest values of $a_j$) and their thresholds sit between $-2.5$ and $0$, so they separates people in the unhealthy half of the distribution. GH and BP discriminate less but their thresholds are further up in the distribution so they separate out the healthy half. LIM\_SENS and COND have the lowest values of $a_j$ and their thresholds go down below $-4.0$ so they prevent the very sick from piling up at a floor.
	
	The underlying health factor $\theta$ is designed to be continuous, unbounded and distributed $N(0,1)$. However, we can map it back into an expected share of the measure's total scale, i.e. summing overall all $J$ items as a share of the maximum possible score(34). We define this as
	\begin{equation}
		h(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J} \sum_{c=2}^{k_j} P(Y_j \ge c \mid \theta)}{\sum_{j=1}^{J} (k_j - 1)},
		\qquad h_i = h(\hat\theta_i), \quad \hat\theta_i = E[\theta \mid \mathbf{y}_i].
	\end{equation}
	The map is monotone, so $\theta$ and $h$ order people identically, but not linear so the variance profile and cost function differ across the two. In practice the $h()$ transformation compacts the health scores at the healthy end of the distribution and spreads them out more at the unhealthy end. Figure \ref{fig:h_theta_comp} compares the measures and illustrates how they relate to one another. 
	
	The $h$ score is close to a weighted sum of the coded answers: regressing $h$ on the standardised codes gives $R^2 = 0.993$. The parallel is not perfect for two reasons: the uneven gaps between the categories of an ordered item; and an item's best and worst categories are open-ended so they only tell us that a person lies beyond the last threshold.\footnote{The latent scale $\theta$ departs is somewhat further from a weighted sun: regressed on the same codes gives $R^2 = 0.925$. Most of the gap comes from the model not scoring an item's categories as equally spaced: letting each category carry its own score raises the $R^2$ to $0.979$. The rest comes from the items discriminating in different parts of the scale, so that a step on one item counts for more where few others still move.} A linear one-factor model fitted to the same eight codes, treated as continuous, gives a score with a Pearson correlation of $0.995$ with $h$ and $0.998$ with that weighted sum, so on the $[0,1]$ scale the graded-response model's practical content is the weighting rather than the functional form.

	
	In addition to $\theta$ and $h$ we construct a Rockwood-style frailty index \citep{mitnitski2001,searle2008}: the share of 31 equal-weight deficits a person has, namely the six SF-12 physical items graded 0 to 1 by response step, each of the eight physical impairment areas, and each of the 17 ever-diagnosed conditions (clinical depression among them), so that 0 is no deficit and higher is frailer (Appendix~\ref{app:frailty}). 7\% of person-waves have none. Following \citet{hosseini2022}, who model the log of frailty, we also report $\log(\text{frailty}+1/31)$: adding one deficit's worth keeps the zeros in the sample, where \citet{hosseini2022} drop them into a separate probit.\footnote{Adding half the smallest positive value, $1/248$, instead puts the zeros so far below the next group that they drive the variance at young ages; with $1/31$ the variance profile is smooth.} Both indices rank people much as $\theta$ and $h$ do (Spearman $0.92$ with each), and the two pairs line up: $h$ behaves like the frailty index and $\theta$ like its log (Section~\ref{subsec:health_moments}).
	
	\textbf{Relation of our health metric to the economics literature.}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Why this measure - the underlying data are ordinal and bounded, so our model respects this.
		\item Factor methods are common in health economics: \citet{poterba2013,poterba2017} take the first principal component of 27 HRS items, and \citet{blundell2023} of the three subjective health measures common to HRS and ELSA.
		\item In the skill-formation literature, \citet{cunha2010} estimate a closely related dynamic factor model, on continuous test scores that enter linearly in the log of the latent skill.
		\item Item-response models are standard in health measurement: the PROMIS item banks, now widely used in health research and clinical care, were calibrated with the graded-response model \citep{cella2010}, including the 124-item physical function bank \citep{rose2014}.
		\item Item-response models themselves are rare in health economics: \citet{graeber2023} scores latent health from five SOEP items with the graded-response model, \citet{wallace2017} build an item-response index from 21 HRS items, and \citet{meijer2011} estimate the same ordinal factor model on SHARE without calling it one. They are common in the economics of education, for scaling and linking test scores \citep{dashammer2005,daszajonc2010,singh2015,muralidharan2019,jacob2016}, and in the measurement of poverty and deprivation \citep{moffitt2016,corsini2020,farcomeni2025,davis2026}.
		\item The closest work to ours in health dynamics uses five-category self-assessed health kept categorical and usually collapsed: to three states by \citet{denardi2025} and \citet{capatina2015}, to a bad-health indicator by \citet{halliday2008}, and kept at five by \citet{contoyannis2004}. But this would really limits our study of dynamics and in particular the timing and targetting of prevention. 
		\item In our case, the choice of methodology relative to a more familiar principal component or linear factor model is innocuous, if we focus on $h$ - a linear factor model estimated on the same data has a Pearson correlation of $0.995$ (Spearman $0.992$) with $h$.
		\item There is a precedent for modelling the log of a bounded health index, the log frailty of \citet{hosseini2022}; on our data $\log(\text{frailty}+1/31)$ has a variance profile by age like $\theta$'s, and the frailty index in levels one like $h$'s.
		Table \ref{tab:corr} shows the correlation with alternative metrics (UKHLS PCS, MCS, raw chronic count). All of these have theoretical disadvantages over our measure, but PCS is closest.
	\end{itemize}
	\note{I think overall conclusion here is that there is no one way to measure health, but for dynamics we kind of need cardinality which we can maybe pin down with costs.} 
	
	As our preferred case, we report $\theta$ in the main text. It is the scale the measurement model is defined on; it is unbounded, so the Gaussian dynamic models of Section~\ref{sec:types} fit it without a ceiling (on $h$ the healthiest class collapses under AR(1) plus ``spike''); and its cost curve in pounds is convex. Appendix~\ref{app:measures} repeats the main-text figures on $h$, the frailty index and log frailty, and the text flags where the measure changes a conclusion.
	
	
	
	In addition to our health metric, we also construct a proxy for healthcare cost based on the healthcare utilisation information in UKHLS.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Per-person-wave estimate of annual NHS cost from in-patient, out-patient and GP contacts, waves 7--15, in 2024/25 prices. UKHLS records the nights spent in hospital as an in-patient over the past year, and in bands the number of GP consultations and hospital out-patient attendances (none, 1--2, 3--5, 6--10, more than 10, which we score 0, 1.5, 4, 8 and 15). Stays for childbirth are left out.
		\item The construct is
		\begin{equation}
		\widehat{C}_{it} \;=\;
		\underbrace{\text{adm}_{it}\bigl[\,c_{\text{short}}\,\mathbf{1}\{n_{it}\le 1\} + c_{\text{bed}}\, n_{it}\,\mathbf{1}\{n_{it}\ge 2\}\bigr]}_{\text{in-patient}}
		\;+\;
		\underbrace{c_{\text{op}} \cdot \widehat{n}^{\text{op}}_{it}}_{\text{out-patient}}
		\;+\;
		\underbrace{c_{\text{gp}} \cdot \widehat{n}^{\text{gp}}_{it}}_{\text{GP}},
		\end{equation}
		with $\text{adm}_{it}$ any in-patient stay and $n_{it}$ the nights in hospital.
		\item The unit costs are national averages for England in 2024/25. The three hospital prices come from NHS England's National Cost Collection \citep{ncc2025}, which costs every admission and attendance in English NHS trusts:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $c_{\text{short}} = \pounds826$, the average non-elective short-stay episode (0--1 nights).
			\item $c_{\text{bed}} = \pounds791$, the average cost per bed day of a non-elective long stay (2 or more nights).\footnote{From the collection's patient-level costs, given in answer to written question 100046 (UK Parliament, 22 December 2025). A non-elective long stay averages \pounds5{,}449 per episode, which is $6.9$ nights at \pounds791. The 2024/25 collection no longer reports excess bed days separately, so we price every night. The cost per bed day of a short stay is almost the same, \pounds806, so in the collection's own figures a stay's cost rises in proportion to its length.}
			\item $c_{\text{op}} = \pounds191$, the average out-patient attendance across consultant-led (\pounds203) and non-consultant-led (\pounds132) attendances and out-patient procedures (\pounds232).
		\end{itemize}
		The GP price is not in the collection. $c_{\text{gp}} = \pounds40$ is a 10-minute surgery consultation, including direct care staff and excluding qualification costs (\pounds48 with them), from PSSRU's \emph{Unit Costs of Health and Social Care 2025} \citep[Table~9.4.2]{pssru2025}.
		\item None of these prices depends on age. The NHS costs an admission by its Healthcare Resource Group (HRG), a group of similar diagnoses or procedures. Some HRGs have separate versions for children (18 and under), but none has an older-age band. Within a condition, however, an admission with more or worse complications and comorbidities falls into a dearer HRG. An older person's stay therefore costs more because they are on average sicker, not because they are older. Our index prices every short stay and every night the same, so it picks up severity only through length of stay and misses the rest of this gradient. The convexity of cost in poor health that we report is therefore probably understated.
		\item Pricing every night at the average works the other way for the longest stays, whose later nights probably cost less than the first: 42\% of reported nights fall in years with more than 30 nights in hospital. A two-part price with the same \pounds5{,}449 for the average long stay, a fixed \pounds3{,}038 per stay plus \pounds350 a night, lowers the sickest-to-healthiest ratio from $18.6$ to $13.9$ but leaves the convexity intact (quadratic $t = 22.4$; Appendix~\ref{app:cost}).
		\item The index averages about \pounds900 a year, against NHS spending of roughly \pounds3{,}000 per head, as it leaves out prescribing, A\&E, community services and social care. It is wrong in level but roughly right in \emph{shape}, and the variation of cost is all we need. Underlying data, construction and validation against published statistics are in Appendix~\ref{app:cost}.
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	Figure \ref{fig:cost} shows that:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Cost rises convexly with poor health: the quadratic term in standardised $\theta$, person-clustered, has $t = 17.4$ ($33.5$ on costs capped at the 99th percentile). A cubic term steepens the sick end further. It is significant on the cost index ($t=-4.5$) and, with a much smaller coefficient, on capped costs ($t=-2.3$). Section~\ref{sec:policy} prices with the cubic, drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:cost}(a), and Appendix~\ref{app:returns_capped} with the capped fit, which is a quadratic.\footnote{A quadratic coefficient is driven by the top of the cost distribution, so we also report results on costs capped at the 99th percentile. The cost index is uncapped throughout unless we say it is capped.}
	\item Over most of the range the relationship is close to age-invariant. The binned curves for the three age bands lie on top of one another down to $\theta \approx -2$; in the sickest pooled tenth mean cost is \pounds4{,}075 at 20--34 and \pounds4{,}392 at 75--90; and the quadratic term is positive within every one of six age bands. Beyond $-2$ the over-65s cost more. The 75--90 band's curvature is roughly double the others' ($t = 4.4$) and still about 40\% higher when costs are capped at the 99th percentile ($t = 3.6$). Longer stays at the same measured health could reflect severity that $\theta$ misses, or slower discharge.\footnote{The linear term's interaction with age is insignificant on $\theta$ in every specification we try ($|t| \le 1.7$).}
	\item Mortality also rises steeply with poor health, roughly log-linearly: pooled across ages with a quadratic in age, the log-odds of death fall by $0.90$ per standard deviation of $\theta$.

\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item From panel (a) the mean accelerates down: $\theta$ falls by about $0.014$ standard deviations a year between 30 and 50, $0.020$ between 50 and 70, and $0.038$ after 70. $\E(d)$ rises with age, which on its own says intervene late: the mean channel of $P(r)$ is largest where the preventable decline is largest.
		\item From panel (b) the variance rises by about 60\% between 30 and 60, peaks at 62, and falls by a fifth by 85. Under stationary noise this says the local covariance $C_{Hd}(a)$ has reached zero around 60 and turned negative after it: interventions on dispersion pay early.
		\item From panel (c) we see divergence then convergence: every row drops sharply in its first year, by 40\% at base age 20 and about 20\% at 50--60 (the one-year ``spike'', health not carried even one year). After this the younger rows run flatter while the older rows fall more steeply.
		\item The shape of the variance profile depends on the measure as Section~\ref{sec:data} anticipated (Appendix~\ref{app:measures}).
		\item On $\log(\text{frailty}+1/31)$ the pattern is very similar, with variance rising by a factor of $1.70$ from 30 to 60 and falling to $0.70$ of its 60 level by 85, against $1.62$ and $0.78$ on $\theta$.
		\item On $h$ and the frailty index, variance continues to increase after 60, but at a markedly slower rate. The bounded measures keep dispersion growing into old age; the unbounded ones have it peak around 60 and then compress.
		\item Across all three alternative indices, panels (a) and (c) show the same stylised facts.
		\item One stationary noise process cannot produce both. Either the noise moves with age or the local $C_{Hd}$ has changed sign: divergence among the young, compression among the old. 
		\item This has implications for timing and targeting: intervene early and target the low early in life; later in life the covariance turns against targeting on levels so not much use targetting on low.
		\item Cautions carried to the next section: composition (who survives to be measured), floors and cohort pooling are all in these curves; the type models will use within-person information.
		\item The covariance rows in Figure \ref{fig:exhibit1} are based on pairwise covariances. Covariance rows on balanced samples, i.e. each row over the people observed at all of its ages, are shown in Appendix~\ref{app:descriptive} (Figure~\ref{fig:ex1balanced}).
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Why and how}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The health-dynamics literature generally works with the old (HRS, ELSA) or short windows; \citet{denardi2025} splice the PSID early in life with the HRS later, but health is modelled only for the older people; \citet{hosseini2022} build a frailty index on the PSID. 
		\item The prevention framework says $H_{55}$ is an \emph{outcome} of the dynamics before 55, not an input. And the data say the dynamics differ across the lifecycle (Figure~\ref{fig:exhibit1}): starting in late middle age misses the divergence phase. So this is why we focus on the full lifecycle.
		\item \textbf{How.} Overlapping cohorts observed over 15 waves: cohort-independent types with cohort-dependent shares. First non-parametric (partial K-means), then a parametric refinement (Bayesian mixtures) that separates persistent deviations from one-period ``spikes'', then against observables. Everything is on $\theta$; the $h$ versions, and the K-means types on the two frailty indices, are in the appendix.
		\item \textbf{Four challenges} addressed along the way: (1) power and type categorisation (functional forms, stochastic structure, several dimensions); (2) mortality and attrition; (3) cohort effects; (4) units and bounds (ceiling, floor, cardinalisation), which is why we work on $\theta$ and repeat the analysis on the bounded measures in the appendix.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{(A) Non-parametric types: partial K-means}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Method: Included respondents with at least three observed ages between 20 and 89 (38,963), the same rows as the Bayesian mixtures below. Each person is a vector over ages 20-90 with most entries missing. 
		\item Estimate type/cluster by minimising average squared deviations over observed entries only - ``partial" K-meanrs. 50 seeded starts to insure that results are not sensitive to initialisation. 
		\item Baseline case is $K=3$. 
		\item ``Equal penalty'': every person-wave is punished equally, so the method is neutral about whether the stochastic process is transitory or persistent. \note{Open: the person is assigned to one cluster for life, which does impose that the type is permanent, so that does mean that the within person dynamics drive the results.}
		\item We focus on means because they are what the policy objects use and what the interventions considered shift.
	\end{itemize}

	Figure~\ref{fig:clusters} shows the cluster results on $\theta$; $h$, the two frailty indices and predicted cost are in Appendix~\ref{app:measures}.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item On $\theta$, three ordered, non-crossing paths, shares 18 / 43 / 40\%. The worst type starts below the others at 20 and falls fastest to 60, by 1.2 standard deviations against 0.4 for the best type; after 60 the order reverses, the worst type falling 0.3 by 85 and the other two about 0.8. Divergence and then convergence is visible in the type paths themselves.
		\item The choice of health metric matters a bit (Appendix~\ref{app:measures}), but the overall shape looks very similar across each of the four we consider.
		\item On $h$ K-means isolates a 10\% sick tail (shares 10 / 28 / 61\%) instead of splitting the healthy majority, and agrees with the $\theta$ types at ARI $0.37$ (71\% in the same type): $\theta$ stretches the healthy end. The frailty index lands with $h$ (ARI $0.61$, 86\%) and log frailty with $\theta$ (ARI $0.44$, 78\%, shares 21 / 41 / 38\%).
		\item The stochastic part (middle panel): within-type deviations are persistent, with lag-1 autocorrelation $0.64$ in the worst type and $0.37$--$0.38$ elsewhere, decaying by nine years to $0.24$, $0.16$ and $0.03$ (the middle type). Suggests that a final model should include some persistence, and that it might differ by type.
		\item Composition: on $\theta$ the worst type's share of observed person-waves is 22\% at 20, falls to 13\% at 50 and recovers to 18\% by 70. This is selection into the observed sample, not people changing type. \note{And for $\theta$ in the AR(1)+spike model, this pattern does somewhat reverse and we get a falling share of the unhealthy type by age.}
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{(B) Parametric types: Bayesian mixtures} \label{subsec:parametric}
	
	The parametric counterpart of the K-means types is a finite mixture of quadratic growth curves. Let $y_{ia}$ be the measure of person $i$ at age $a$, standardised on the contract, and $\tilde a=(a-55)/10$. Person $i$ belongs to one of $K$ classes, $k_i\in\{1,\dots,K\}$, with $\Pr(k_i=k)=\pi_k$, and in the baseline specification
	\begin{equation}
		y_{ia}=\alpha_{k_i}+\beta_{k_i}\tilde a+\gamma_{k_i}\tilde a^{2}+\varepsilon_{ia},\qquad \varepsilon_{ia}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})\ \text{i.i.d.},
		\label{eq:mixbase}
	\end{equation}
	so a class is a path and a residual scale common to the classes. The class indicator is marginalised: person $i$'s likelihood is $\sum_k\pi_k\prod_a N\bigl(y_{ia}\mid\mu_k(a),\sigma^2\bigr)$ over their observed ages, and their posterior class probabilities follow from the fitted parameters. Labels are identified by ordering the intercepts, $\alpha_1<\dots<\alpha_K$, so class 1 is worst health. Priors are weakly informative on the standardised scale: $\pi\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(1.5)$, $\alpha_k\sim N(0,1.5^2)$, $\beta_k\sim N(0,1)$, $\gamma_k\sim N(0,0.05^2)$ and $\sigma\sim N(0.7,0.5^2)$ truncated below at $0.05$. Estimation is by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (the no-U-turn sampler) in Stan: four chains of 1,000 warm-up and 1,000 sampling iterations, with convergence judged by the split-$\hat R$ of the structural parameters.
	

	Figure~\ref{fig:bayes}(a) shows the Bayesian equivalent of the partial K-means:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Same data as the partial K-means, fitted on $\theta$; the $h$, frailty-index and log-frailty versions are in Appendix~\ref{app:measures}.
		\item The baseline $K=3$ mixture (quadratic paths, independent residuals, no AR term) converged (max $\hat R = 1.001$, about 3 h).
		\item The mixture and the K-means types are the same typology. Shares agree to a few points ($.15/.44/.41$ against $.18/.43/.40$), the class paths track the type means, and person by person 95\% of people are in the same class (ARI $0.85$, mean maximum posterior $0.93$).
		\item The same holds on $h$ (ARI $0.82$, 94\% in the same class): there both methods isolate a 10\% sick tail, where on $\theta$ both split the healthy majority, so the $h$--$\theta$ split is a property of the scale, not of the method.
		\item The composition strips move the same way under both methods: on $\theta$ the worst class is 13\% of the observed person-waves at 30 and 16\% at 85 under the mixture, against 16\% and 18\% under K-means.
	\end{itemize}
	

	
	The paper's stochastic specification changes only the residual. In place of the i.i.d.\ $\varepsilon_{ia}$ of \eqref{eq:mixbase}, the deviation from the class path is a persistent state plus a one-period ``spike'',
	\begin{equation}
		y_{ia}=\mu_{k_i}(a)+u_{ia}+\varepsilon_{ia},\qquad u_{ia}=\rho_{k_i}^{\,g}\,u_{i,a-g}+\eta_{ia},\quad \eta_{ia}\sim N\Bigl(0,\ \sigma^{2}\tfrac{1-\rho_{k_i}^{2g}}{1-\rho_{k_i}^{2}}\Bigr),\quad \varepsilon_{ia}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),
		\label{eq:mixssm}
	\end{equation}
	where $g$ is the gap in years since the person's previous observation, the state starts from its stationary distribution $u\sim N\bigl(0,\sigma^2/(1-\rho_{k}^2)\bigr)$, and the persistence $\rho_k$ is class-specific while the innovation scale $\sigma$ and the ``spike'' scale $\sigma_\varepsilon$ are common. The two variances are identified off the autocorrelation function, $\rho^{g}$ times the state's share of the variance, so $\rho$ comes from the ratio of successive autocorrelations and the ``spike'' from their level; each person's likelihood is computed by the Kalman filter, class by class, and the rest is as in the baseline. Priors add $\rho_k\sim\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ and $\sigma_\varepsilon\sim N(0,0.4^2)$ truncated below at $0.01$.
	
	Figure~\ref{fig:bayes}(b) shows the AR(1) plus ``spike'' specification:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The same specification on $\theta$ (and on $h$ for the appendix), on the same contract; converged (max $\hat R = 1.002$, 4--8 h).
		\item On $\theta$ the story carries over. Shares $.25/.47/.29$, persistence $.96/.90/.87$, a ``spike'' of $0.42$ against a state innovation of $0.23$: between a fifth and a half of the variance around each class's path is the ``spike'' (signal shares $0.78$, $0.63$ and $0.56$), the worst class starts within half a standard deviation of the middle and falls away, and the composition drift reverses: the worst class is 26\% of the observed person-waves at 30 and 17\% at 85, against $13\%\to16\%$ under the baseline.
		\item Agreement with the K-means types falls to ARI $0.42$ (77\% in the same class), and classification is less certain (mean maximum posterior $0.69$ against $0.93$).
		\item On $h$ (Appendix~\ref{app:measures}) the healthiest class collapses: its persistence is $0.25$, against $0.96$ and $0.78$ for the other two, and agreement with the K-means types falls to ARI $0.46$, with half of K-means type 2 reassigned to the worst class. This bounded-scale problem is one reason we report $\theta$. The frailty index, bounded at zero, does the same at its floor (Figures~\ref{fig:bayes_frailty} and \ref{fig:bayes_logfrailty}): under AR(1) plus ``spike'' its healthiest class has persistence $0.36$ against $0.98$ and $0.81$, and agreement with the K-means types falls to ARI $0.34$; the shifted log lifts the floor only partly, with a small healthiest class ($10\%$) at persistence $0.44$ against $0.96$ and $0.92$ for the other two and ARI $0.29$. Under independent residuals all four measures reproduce their K-means types (ARI $0.81$--$0.89$, at least 94\% of people in the same type). \note{when we go to $K=4$ then we start getting a convergence again though}
		\item The specification is close to that of \citet{hosseini2022}, who model log frailty as a fixed effect plus an AR(1) plus white noise. Our classes play the role of a coarse fixed effect, and their persistence of $0.991$ compares with our $0.87$--$0.96$.
	\end{itemize}

	\paragraph{Is there always a bad group?}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Yes, and it is the same people under every method.} Every typology finds a worst group of 15--18\% by modal label: 18\% under partial K-means, 15\% under independent residuals, 18\% under AR(1) plus ``spike'' and 16\% under the multidimensional fit. Of the K-means worst type, 84\% are worst under independent residuals, 85\% under AR(1) plus ``spike'' and 74\% under the multidimensional fit; the mixtures give the K-means worst people a posterior probability of the worst class of $0.73$--$0.84$ and everyone else $0.00$--$0.13$. The AR(1) plus ``spike'' fit's larger share, 25\%, is probability mass rather than people: a quarter of the population is in the worst class in expectation, 18\% by argmax, because the persistent component leaves 38\% of people with a maximum posterior below $0.6$ (4\% under independent residuals).
		\item \textbf{What the stochastic structure changes is the account of how they got there, not the size of the group} (Appendix~\ref{app:worsttype} for the worst type by method and $K$).
		\item \textbf{The level-slope reversal of Figures~\ref{fig:ex2main} and~\ref{fig:decomp} is this group.} Splitting $\Cov_j(\bar h_{j,a},d_{j,a})$ into each type's term, the worst type's carries nearly all of the negative covariance after 60 under every typology and every $K$, and it is still negative when the slopes are taken from within-person changes rather than the type paths (Appendix~\ref{app:reversal}). Refitting the Case~4 moments without the bottom fifth of the age-standardised person mean, or without either method's worst type, leaves no reversal. The accumulation margin of Section~\ref{sec:framework} turns negative because the unhealthy fifth stops falling away from the rest while the healthy start to fall late, which is also why targeting on the type pays after 60 (Section~\ref{sec:policy}). K-means and the independent-residuals mixture, which classify on levels, find a worst type that is already a full unit of $\theta$ below the others at 20 and whose cross-sectional path flattens after 60. The AR(1) plus ``spike'' fit finds a worst class that starts near the average and declines fastest, because a low level at 20 can be read as a carried deviation rather than as type; it therefore puts the between-type share of the variance at 19\% at 30 where K-means puts it at 48\% (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}), and it keeps the class declining through old age. So K-means does not understate the bad group; it overstates its early separation and understates its decline, and it is the decline, not the level, that the prevention returns of Section~\ref{sec:policy} price. The noisier the process, the more of the early-life gap a level-based method attributes to type.
	\end{itemize}

	\paragraph{Extensions: $K$, dimensions, mortality.}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $K=4$ and $K=5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:trajK}) converge cleanly on every specification (max $\hat R\le1.004$). Under independent residuals each added class is a new sickest group, 10\% of people at $K=4$ and a further 6\% at $K=5$, and the healthy majority is left alone.
		\item Under AR(1) plus ``spike'' the split goes the other way. At $K=4$ the three $K=3$ classes barely move and the new class is a small healthy group, 8\% with persistence $0.70$. At $K=5$ the three large classes are unchanged, with persistence $0.86$--$0.94$, and the two new classes are both small and healthy, 7\% and 6\%, one with persistence $0.07$: a collapse like $h$'s reaches $\theta$ too at $K=5$, but in a small class rather than half the sample. Classification certainty falls from a mean maximum posterior of $0.69$ at $K=3$ to $0.66$ at $K=4$.
		\item On $h$ (Appendix~\ref{app:measures}) the healthiest-class collapse spreads rather than resolves as classes are added: the top two classes have persistence $0.52$ and $0.19$ at $K=4$, and $0.39$ and $0.18$ at $K=5$, covering about half the sample.
		\item Multidimensional model (Figure~\ref{fig:multidim}): the physical measure ($\theta$; $h$ in Appendix~\ref{app:measures}) and a mental GRM (the three SF-12 mental testlets and two GHQ-12 testlets, no depression diagnosis; Appendix~\ref{app:mental} looks at it on its own) as Gaussian channels, plus mortality, one shared class structure; the health contract's people with a mental score on every row, 38,181 people and 2,045 deaths. Under AR(1) plus ``spike'' the persistence is class and channel specific while the innovation and ``spike'' scales are common to the classes; the hazard is Gompertz--Makeham, $\lambda_k(a)=c+b_k e^{\vartheta_k(a-55)}$, integrated over each row's years at risk, with class-specific level and slope and a common Makeham constant. The figure is the fit with one level shift per birth decade on both channels, common to the classes, drawn at the 1950s: without the shifts (Appendix~\ref{app:cohort}) the mental class paths rise with age, which the shifts attribute to the older cohorts' higher level, with the caveat on reading them as cohort effects set out below.
		\item The physical typology survives the extra channels. The classes are $.18/.50/.32$ with physical persistence $.94/.90/.86$, the same to two decimals as without the decade shifts ($.19/.51/.31$ and $.94/.91/.87$), and 76\% of people land in the same class as under the single-channel fit without shifts (73\% against the single-channel fit with them, Figure~\ref{fig:multidim_vs_uni}). On $h$ (Appendix~\ref{app:measures}) the healthiest class keeps its collapse, persistence $0.28$, even though its mental persistence is $0.86$.
		\item The decade shifts change nothing else either: the same ``spike'' scales, a class-1 to class-3 hazard ratio of $11.5$ at 55 and $3.2$ at 85 against $10.6$ and $3.3$ without them, and a held-out twin that predicts exactly as the fit without shifts does (Figure~\ref{fig:predmort} and Section~\ref{subsec:pred}: $R^2$ $0.61$ and $0.74$ for $\theta_{t+1}$ from the classes and the forecast, against $0.61$ and $0.74$). The shifts themselves fall monotonically from the 1920s to the 2000s, by $0.9$ sd on $\theta$ and $1.3$ sd on the mental score.
		\item Mental health is faster and noisier than physical health in every class: persistence $.93/.89/.82$ against $.94/.90/.86$ on $\theta$, and a ``spike'' of $0.54$ against $0.42$, so the signal share of the mental channel is $0.45$--$0.66$ against $0.55$--$0.74$. A shared persistence across channels, as in earlier versions, would have been wrong. At the 1950s the worst physical class sits a standard deviation below the best on mental health at 55, and every class's mental path declines with age once the decade shifts are taken out.
		\item Mortality is strongly graded by class and converges with age: the yearly hazard of the worst class is 11.5 times the best class's at 55 and 3.2 times at 85 (10.6 and 3.3 without the shifts; 7.1 and 2.7 under independent residuals), the sickest class having the flattest Gompertz slope ($0.09$ against $0.14$ per year). The Makeham constant is below $10^{-4}$ in every fit. Mortality describes survival by class; it does not define the classes. This is where the survivor-bias discussion lives.
		\item Note that in the AR(1) plus ``spike'' case, the class composition by age now looks like it reflects mortality selection. We need to look into this a bit more, but it is certainly promising!
		\item \textbf{Windows} (Figure~\ref{fig:windows}; \code{descriptives/50\_paper\_windows\_bayes.py}): the AR(1) plus ``spike'' mixture refitted on twenty-year windows and on windows expanding backwards from 90, each on its own contract, compared with the lifecycle classes by the co-assignment correlation (over pairs of people, the correlation between the probability that the window fit puts the pair in the same class and the probability that the lifecycle fit does; it needs no label correspondence and is 1 whenever the two posteriors coincide, however diffuse). The sliding windows agree at $0.68$--$0.88$ up to 60--80 and $0.42$ at 70--90; the backward windows at $0.75$ from 60, $0.86$ from 50, $0.95$ from 40 and $0.99$ from 30. Short windows blur the posteriors more than they regroup people: the adjusted Rand index of the modal classes is far lower ($0.23$ for 20--40 against $0.68$ on the correlation), which is the modal label becoming a coin toss among the healthy young, not a different typology.
		\item \textbf{A window finds the bad type only if it contains the years in which that type diverges.} The worst class is not the same object in every window. Its share of a window's people runs 15, 22, 33, 8, 16 and 16\% across the sliding windows: the 40--60 window finds a worst class a third larger than the lifecycle one (32\% of the person-waves at 40--60 against 24\%), because that is where the decline happens and a path model separates the decliners there, while every window starting at 50 or later finds a small, flat, very sick class (7--14\% of the person-waves at 60--85 against 18--22\%) and puts the late decliners into a middle class of 50--80\%. The backward windows say the same from the other side: the worst class is 16--17\% in the 70--90 and 60--90 windows and 14\% in 50--90, then 30\% once the window reaches back to 40 and 27\% from 30. The bad type is not growing because a later window's people are older; it appears when the window reaches back far enough to contain the divergence, which sits between 40 and 60. A level is visible at any age, a slope needs exposure (Section~\ref{sec:ident_fw}), and about fifty years of ages, most of them the 40--60 decade, is what the types need.
		\item At the oldest ages three classes are not supported: the 70--90 window's third class is 3\% of people with persistence $0.09$, the mixture wanting two classes and a ``spike''. That is the one window where the correlation is low for a real reason. The partial K-means version (Figure~\ref{fig:windows_kmeans}) is more stable across windows ($0.58$--$0.95$) and its worst type's share moves with the age composition of the window's people, but that stability is the method's insensitivity to where the decline happens: K-means classifies on person-by-age profiles, the mixture on paths with persistence, and only the latter can tell a late decliner from a lifelong low type.
		\item \textbf{Cohort effects.} The AR(1) plus ``spike'' fits, single-channel and multidimensional, were repeated with one level shift per birth decade, common to the classes (Appendix~\ref{app:cohort}). With fifteen waves the panel observes each person over at most a fifteen-year span, so a cohort's level is identified from the overlap of neighbouring decades at the ages they share.
		\item \textbf{The typology is unaffected.} Shares, persistence and the ``spike'' scale agree with the fits without cohort effects to two or three decimals on every variant, and the class paths keep their shape; the worst class is a little steeper once the decade shifts carry part of the level. Whatever cohort variation there is, it is not what separates the classes.
		\item \textbf{The cohort effects themselves should not be read strongly.} The fit reports a monotone decline between the 1950s and the 2000s of about two thirds of a standard deviation on $\theta$ (a third on $h$). But the raw decade profiles line up almost exactly at the ages they share, and subtracting the estimated shifts opens gaps that were not there. Over a fifteen-year window age and birth year are nearly collinear, and a decade dummy is close to a linear function of age within it, so with a single quadratic age path per class the dummies absorb the curvature the quadratic cannot follow. With single-year age dummies in place of the quadratic, the pre-1960 shifts vanish and only the post-1970 cohorts decline (Appendix~\ref{app:kmeans_cohort}), so the mixture's shifts should not be read as cohort effects.
		\item \textbf{Specification.} The same $K=3$ fits with an AR(1) in the class residual but no ``spike'' give persistence of $0.79/0.28/0.40$ on $\theta$, against $0.87$--$0.96$ once the one-period term is separated: without it $\rho$ has to absorb the one-period noise and is pulled down.
		\item \todo{still to do for the bounded measures in the appendix: a bounded observation model (scores at the ceiling of $h$ or the floor of frailty treated as censored).}
	\end{itemize}

	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item Can the classes predict death, rather than only describe survival by class? The held-out design of Section~\ref{subsec:pred} cannot answer this at a fixed horizon: everyone at $t$ has two more rows, and $t+2$ is by construction everyone's last interview. Utilisation is the opposite case (Section~\ref{subsec:pred}): health does nearly all the work there, so prevention targeted on these measures should expect to move costs far more readily than mortality.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Predictive value} \label{subsec:pred}
	
	Broadly two types of exercise here - hold out the last two observed rows, or hold out the first two observed rows. The first tells us how valuable/accurate these clusters are at predicting future developments, and the second tells us how easy cluster membership is to predict based on health and other observables. Headline result is that health is what matters, the model helps a bit, and observables are almost irrelevant.
	
	In order to run these exercises, we use people with at least five rows, so that every fit keeps at least three after two are held out: 26,664 people with at least five rows in both the single-channel and the multidimensional samples for predicting ahead, and 29,899 with at least five rows of health for predicting the type (29,278 for the multidimensional types).
	
	
	First exercise - predicting the future: 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Fit model on the smaller sample and held out the final two observations for each person, so $t$ is a person's final in-sample observation and out-of-sample predictions can be made for $t+1$ and $t+2$.
		\item Predict two things: health and the cost proxy, using only information up to $t$. 
		\item In each case, baseline is a regression of quadratic in age on the outcome, estimated on 70\% on individuals and predicted on 30\%. The extra information is then added as a predictor to this regression. 
		\item Key results are:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Observables predict basically nothing.
			\item Current health and the class contain about the same amount, but the forecast of the model does slightly better (but still only sligthly better than having two past health observations).  
			\item The multidimensional classes and forecast match the single-channel ones, because the multidimensional model is dominated by the phsical health channel.
			\item Ranking is about the same for $t+1$ and $t+2$,
		\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	Second exercise - predicting types:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Fit model on the smaller sample but held out the first two observations for each person, so $t$ is a person's first in-sample observation and so their type can be predicted using information from $t-1$ and $t-2$.
		\item Observables basically don't predict type (panel b), but there are clear and intuitive differences between the types (panel d). 
		\item One health reading is by far the most valuable, and a second one helps a bit as well, but really not much.
		\item Health is the weakest guide to type when young: the balanced accuracy of the AR(1) plus ``spike'' type from $\theta_{t-1}$ rises from 0.43 at ages 20--34 to 0.60 at 35--49 and 0.72 at 50--64, then eases to 0.67 at 65--90 (0.58, 0.65, 0.70, 0.66 for K-means; by the age the reading was taken). Predicting ahead has the same shape (panel c), peaking at 50--64; trajectories have not yet fanned out when people are young.
		\item Footnote comparing to $h$ - $\theta$ does much better because $h$ ends up really bunched at the ceiling while $\theta$ has a more balanced distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:predsummary_h}). 
	\end{itemize}

	We price the returns of Section~\ref{sec:framework} on the $K=3$ AR(1) plus ``spike'' classes on $\theta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:bayes}(b)), with the independent-residuals classes alongside for comparison. The cost function is the curve estimated in pounds on $\theta$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cost}), a cubic fitted to the cost index over waves 7--15, and the returns take the general-cost form of Section~\ref{sec:policy_fw}. Class shares and paths are the fitted mixture at its posterior mean; $\E[-c'(\theta)\mid j]$ at each age is the posterior-weighted mean over the person-waves observed there, and the bill $C(r)$ and its three parts are the observed cross-section priced by the same curve. Returns are pounds of treatment cost to 90 avoided, per unit of $\mu$ to first order and exactly at $\mu=1$ \eqref{eq:Sexact}, with no survival weighting and no discounting. The K-means version and the capped cost curve are in Appendix~\ref{app:returns}; the same exercise on $h$ is in Appendix~\ref{app:measures}.
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{The bill and what prevention avoids} (Figure~\ref{fig:prevention}(a), (b)). The treatment bill from 25 is \pounds67{,}500 per person to 90, \pounds53{,}000 from 50 and \pounds33{,}000 from 70. On the AR(1) plus ``spike'' classes about 63\% of it is the cost along the mean path, 16\% is what the type differences add and 21\% is realised deviations, the part no prevention reaches (19\% from 50, 17\% from 70). Uniform prevention from 25 avoids \pounds79{,}200 per unit of $\mu$ to first order, so $P(r)/C(r)$ is $1.17$ at 25, falling to $0.78$ at 50 and $0.45$ at 70. $P(r)$ is the saving per unit of $\mu$ as $\mu$ starts to rise from zero, so on a convex cost curve it is not bounded by the bill: each unit of health restored is worth less once the person is healthier. Because the curve is a cubic, \eqref{eq:Sexact} gives the saving at any $\mu$ exactly: from 25 it is $\mu\,(79{,}200-46{,}200\,\mu+10{,}700\,\mu^2)$ pounds, so the first-order return overstates the saving per unit of $\mu$ by 6\% at $\mu=0.1$, 16\% at $0.25$, 35\% at $0.5$ and 81\% at $\mu=1$. Holding back the whole decline ($\mu=1$) saves \pounds43{,}700 from 25, 65\% of the bill, and 52\% from 50 and 35\% from 70. Either way, the later the start, the larger the share of the remaining bill that is already determined. On the independent-residuals classes $P(r)/C(r)$ is $0.88$, $0.54$ and $0.29$, and $S(r)/C(r)$ is $0.56$, $0.39$ and $0.24$, since more of the decline is read as noise. \todo{Note here the difference between the $\mu=0.1$ case which is basically the first order approx, and teh full bill in panel (b), which tails off because of the convexity in health costs. }

From 25 the worst class returns more than twice the uniform return (\pounds184{,}400 against \pounds79{,}200 per unit of $\mu$ to first order; \pounds17{,}400 against \pounds7{,}500 at $\mu=0.1$) because its gap and its decline coincide, already below the others at 25 and falling fastest; the middle class returns a little less than the uniform amount (\pounds66{,}900); the best class, healthy and nearly flat, a tenth of it (\pounds8{,}400). Once the one-period noise is absorbed, the classes fan out for longer, and divergence keeps working for uniform prevention into old age.

From 50 the worst class still has the highest return and the gain is 38\%; from 70 the middle class does, and the gain is 51\%. The exact saving at $\mu=1$ gives the same ranking, with gains of 135\%, 64\% and 45\%. On the independent-residuals classes the gains are 193\%, 61\% and 53\% (210\%, 93\% and 51\% at $\mu=1$).

	When do we get the savings?
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Figure~\ref{fig:prevention}(c) shows that the actual savings of prevention are generally backloaded, but less so for the worst class.  The worst class pays back soonest (half by 72 from a start at 25), the middle class later (82), because its decline comes late, and the best class's small return almost entirely in old age (86). The saving per year is still rising at 90, so the horizon matters too.
		\item Given this mismatch of the cost and benefit of prevention, discounting is likely to bite hard. We haven't looked into this in detail yet. \todo{Could also add the cost of a unit of $\mu$ and the survival weighting; both change the timing arithmetic.}
	\end{itemize}
	
	What should we target on?
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The class returns in panels (a)-(c) assume that the class is known, but of course in practice it wouldn't be.
		\item Figure~\ref{fig:prevention}(d) uses the version with the first two observations held out to show the value of targetting based only on known information. The rules are fitted on 70\% of them and scored on the other 30\%, so the two readings they predict from never shaped those classes.
		\item In each case it shows the return to prevention at $\mu=0.1$ when targetting based on information known about that person at time $t-1$, just before their fitted window starts at $t$ ($\theta_{t-1}$, then $\theta_{t-1}$ and $\theta_{t-2}$, as in Figure~\ref{fig:predsummary}). The estimated class is the posterior from the person's whole fitted window (so excluding the first two observations).
		\item People are treated from their own age at $t-1$, not from 25, so the first result is that targetting by age is obviously much more effective than uniform prevention (at 10\% coverage, \pounds7{,}600 per treated person against \pounds4{,}500 for everyone).
		\item After age, the health reading is the information that matters: at 10\% coverage, adding education, income and sex raises the saving to \pounds8{,}300 per treated person, $\theta_{t-1}$ to \pounds9{,}100, and $\theta_{t-1}$ and $\theta_{t-2}$ to \pounds9{,}300, against \pounds10{,}800 for the estimated class.
		\item The remaining gap to the estimated class is what a longer health history buys: the class is estimated from a person's whole fitted window, and two readings close about half of the gap between age alone and it (51\%, against 47\% for one reading and 20\% for the observables).
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Health variables} \label{app:health_vars}
	
	Six SF-12 items enter as four testlets: the two physical-function items sum into PF, the two role-physical items sum into RP, and general health and pain enter alone. \note{add explanation of relationship to PCS here.} Every item is coded so category 1 is worst
	health. In addition to the SF-12 items, UKHLS asks which of a list of areas a person's health limits them in. Eight of these are physical and used here, in three groups:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item LIM\_FL (0-4): mobility (\code{disdif1}), lifting (\code{disdif2}), dexterity (\code{disdif3}), co-ordination (\code{disdif10}
		\item LIM\_SELF (0-2): continence (\code{disdif4}), personal care (\code{disdif11}) 
		\item LIM\_SENS (0-2): hearing (\code{disdif5}), sight (\code{disdif6}) 
	\end{enumerate}
	In the early waves the battery was asked only of people reporting a long-standing illness. Someone who reports none therefore has no answer, not a zero. We count them as having no limitation, in every wave, so that the variable means the same thing across the panel; someone who does report a long-standing illness but has no usable answer is missing. Two areas are deliberately left out. ``Recognising when in physical danger'' is included with the mental health items and ``Other health problem or disability'' is included as a robustness variant and changes nothing. COND counts the sixteen ever-diagnosed physical conditions in the UKHLS inventory, 0 to 5 and 6 or more, the top category being the highest count whose share of person-waves is at least $0.5\%$ (6 or more holds $0.86\%$).

	\subsection{The frailty indices} \label{app:frailty}
	
	The frailty index counts 31 deficits, each scored 0 to 1 and weighted equally: the six SF-12 physical items (general health, moderate activities, climbing stairs, accomplished less, limited in kind, pain), graded by response step; the eight physical impairment areas of Section~\ref{app:health_vars} (mobility, lifting, dexterity, co-ordination, continence, personal care, hearing, sight), each 0 or 1; and the 17 ever-diagnosed conditions of the UKHLS inventory (asthma, arthritis, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, stroke, emphysema, hyper- and hypothyroidism, chronic bronchitis, liver condition, cancer, diabetes, epilepsy, high blood pressure, clinical depression), each 0 or 1. The index is the share of deficits present, so higher is frailer. 7\% of person-waves have none: 12\% in the twenties, 6\% in the fifties and under 1\% past 80.
	
	Log frailty is $\log(\text{frailty}+1/31)$. The shift matters mainly at young ages, where the zeros are: with half the smallest positive value, $1/248$, the zeros sit so far below the next group up that the variance at 25 is $1.15$ and the profile peaks at 48, against $0.29$ and a peak at 61 with $1/31$.
	
	The index differs from that of \citet{hosseini2022} in three ways. They score 28 deficits, all 0/1, including high BMI, smoking and mental and cognitive impairments; they exclude self-rated health; and they model the zeros with a separate probit rather than shifting them.

	UKHLS gives three tiers of contact, not two: \code{hl2gp} is ``Visited GP in last 12 months'' and \code{hl2hop} ``Hospital or clinic out-patient in last 12 months'', both banded $0$--$4$ across waves 7--15. (An earlier draft had the two swapped; the labels were re-checked directly in waves g, j and o.) Unit costs are the 2024/25 national averages set out in Section~\ref{sec:data}: \pounds826 for a stay of 0--1 nights and \pounds791 a night for a longer one (the non-elective rates of the National Cost Collection), \pounds191 per out-patient attendance and \pounds40 per GP consultation (PSSRU). The survey does not say whether a stay was planned, so every stay is priced at the non-elective rates. An elective in-patient episode averages \pounds6{,}862 (\pounds1{,}198 a bed day) and a day case \pounds1{,}078, so this understates the cost of planned treatment.

	A stay of 0--1 nights is costed at the short-stay rate and a longer one at the bed-day rate for every night; out-patient attendances and GP visits at their flat rates, with band midpoints $0, 1.5, 4, 8, 15$. Maternity admissions are separable. On ages 20--90 the flat index averages \pounds908 per person-year: \pounds510 in-patient, \pounds290 out-patient, \pounds109 GP.
	
	Pricing every night also settles what was the main modelling decision under spell-plus-excess-bed-day prices. UKHLS gives total nights but not the number of spells, so a person with 20 nights might have had one long admission or four short ones. At a price per night the two cost the same. The spell count matters only in the two-part check below, which charges a fixed cost per stay; there, assuming one stay per five nights rather than one per year raises the sickest-to-healthiest ratio from $13.9\times$ to $20.0\times$ (Figure~\ref{fig:costconvex}(c)).
	
	For the nights carrying a condition attribution, the flat rate is replaced by a condition-specific one, mapping UKHLS condition code to NCC HRG chapter to average cost, at chapter level since the survey identifies a condition and not a procedure. Unattributed nights take the case-mix-weighted average of the attributed ones \emph{within the same decile of the health measure}, never a global average, which would flatten exactly the gradient the index is for. Attribution reaches 18.0\% of admissions pooled over waves 7--15, below the 29\% on wave 15 alone because \code{hospcp}/\code{hospdcp} begin at wave 11. Weighting moves the mean to \pounds1{,}042, about 16\%. More useful is what it does not do: the imputed condition weight is nearly flat across deciles of physical health, $1.16$ to $1.31$. Sicker people are not admitted for systematically more expensive conditions; they are admitted more often and for longer. Condition weighting is a level adjustment, not a shape one.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_cost_convexity}
		\caption{Cost index in pounds against the four health measures ($\theta$, $h$, and the frailty index and log frailty negated): by decile, per standard deviation of each measure, and the sensitivity of the convexity to every costing assumption the index rests on. Measures oriented so higher means better health. Panel (c) varies the pricing of stays (every night at the bed-day cost, or a fixed cost per stay plus \pounds350 a night), the spell rule, maternity treatment, condition weighting and top-band value, each with a winsorised-at-the-99th-percentile twin.}
		\label{fig:costconvex}
	\end{figure}
	
	\textbf{The costing assumptions do not change the sign, and neither does the health measure.} All twelve variants of the sensitivity grid (every night at the bed-day cost, or a fixed cost per stay plus \pounds350 a night with one stay or one per five nights; maternity in or out; flat or condition-weighted; top band at 15 or 30) are convex in pounds on $\theta$, with person-clustered $t$ between $17$ and $36$. Winsorising at the 99th percentile roughly halves the magnitude and changes nothing else. The sickest-to-healthiest ratio ranges from $10.5\times$ to $20.8\times$, and the pricing of long stays moves it most: $18.6\times$ when every night costs \pounds791, $13.9\times$ under the two-part price. Convexity also holds on every health measure in the figure: $t = 10.4$ on $h$, $20.0$ on log frailty and $6.5$ on the frailty index in levels, against $17.4$ on $\theta$. What the horizontal measure sets is the size of the bend per standard deviation, not its sign: the quadratic term is \pounds443 on $\theta$ against \pounds282 on $h$, \pounds517 on log frailty and \pounds182 on the frailty index, and \pounds207 against \pounds87 on $\theta$ and $h$ on capped costs.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_cost_age_interaction}
		\caption{The cost index in pounds against health within age bands. Panel (a) cost across pooled percentile bins of health, one line per age band; (b) the same table read down the other way; (c) the ratio of the sickest to the healthiest tenth; (d) the slope per standard deviation of $\theta$ and of $h$ over the full range; (e) the ratio of the oldest to the youngest band's slope within windows of the pooled distribution; (f) the share of each band falling in the sickest pooled tenth. Panels (a)--(c) and (f) are the same on every health scale.}
		\label{fig:costage}
	\end{figure}
	
	\textbf{In pounds, the health--cost relationship is close to age-invariant except at the sick end.} At a fixed level of health the index is nearly flat in age over most of the range, shallow-U-shaped with a midlife minimum: the sickest tenth costs \pounds4{,}075 at 20--34 and \pounds4{,}392 at 75--90, and the sickest-to-healthiest ratio is $15$--$21\times$ at every age. The full-range gradient rises, \pounds670 to \pounds1{,}613 per standard deviation of $\theta$ and \pounds1{,}010 to \pounds1{,}476 per standard deviation of $h$. In a pooled quadratic whose slope and curvature both interact with age, the 75--90 band's curvature is roughly double the others' ($t=4.4$ and $3.4$), and on $h$ the slope also flattens with age ($t=-3.9$); on costs capped at the 99th percentile the slope does not change with age ($t=0.7$ on $\theta$, $-1.1$ on $h$) but the curvature still rises with it ($t=3.6$ and $2.3$). Below $\theta=-2$ the over-65s cost more than the young, because they are admitted more often and stay longer once admitted (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Most of the effect of ageing is still that it moves people down the curve: 2.5\% of the 20--34 band is in the sickest pooled tenth against 24.7\% of the 75--90 band. Two cautions: the age profile is understated more than twofold, and UKHLS does not follow people into care homes, so the sickest old are under-represented in a way the sickest young are not.
	
	
	\subsubsection{Validation of cost proxy against published profiles}
	\label{app:costval}
	
	The utilisation sample is $301{,}390$ person-waves on $59{,}750$ people, waves 7--15; the validation restricts to ages 20--90, $283{,}852$ person-waves. Three checks against what is independently known about NHS spending, none of which the index was fitted to.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_cost_validation}
		\caption{Validation of the flat-rate index. Panel (a) the age profile of the whole index and of each tier, relative to the 50s, against the published all-care ratio; (b) the share of each age band top-coded at ``more than ten'' contacts, with the top-band sensitivity; (c) the concentration curve against the published top-decile share; (d) the gradient in physical health, the quantity the index is for.}
		\label{fig:costval}
	\end{figure}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Concentration passes.} The top 1\% of person-waves hold 36.2\% of the total, the top 5\% 61.5\% and the top decile 74.2\%, against a published benchmark of more than half. If anything the index is over-concentrated, which is expected: 22.7\% of person-waves are exactly zero on an index that carries no prescribing, A\&E or community contact, the components that would give almost everyone a small positive amount.
		\item \textbf{Level undershoots, as predicted.} \pounds908 per person-year against NHS spend per head near \pounds3{,}000. Three tiers of contact priced at national averages cannot add to the national total; the shortfall is not informative.
		\item \textbf{The age profile strains.} The whole index puts the 80+ band at $3.0\times$ the 50s, against a published all-care ratio near $7\times$. By tier: in-patient $4.3\times$, out-patient $1.7\times$, GP $1.4\times$. The one tier measured as a true count is the one with a credible gradient; the two banded tiers are nearly flat in age. The bands are not the reason: top-coding does rise with age (7.4\% of the 80+ band at ``more than ten'' GP visits against 4.9\% of the 50s), but scoring the top band at 30 contacts rather than 15 moves the whole-index ratio from $2.97\times$ to $2.85\times$, the wrong way, because it loads more weight onto the tiers that are flat in age. The binding limitation is that cost per contact is held fixed: an out-patient attendance for an 85-year-old and one for a 30-year-old enter at the same \pounds191. That is what linkage to administrative records would fix.
	\end{itemize}
	
	The index's purpose is the gradient in \emph{health}, and there it is strong: $18.6\times$ between the least and most healthy decile on the whole index, $49.0\times$ on the in-patient tier, against an age gradient of $3.0\times$. The caveat is that the age profile is understated by more than a factor of two, so the index should not be used for anything whose answer depends on the age gradient: lifetime cost projections, discounting, or the timing of prevention returns. For those, report the in-patient tier alone or wait for linkage.

	\subsection{Cohort adjustment}
	\label{app:kmeans_cohort}
	
	The partial K-means types are fitted to person-by-age profiles pooled across birth cohorts, so a cohort born later that reports worse health at a given age could be sorted into a worse type for that reason alone. The check has three steps, on the clustering sample and separately for each variant.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Regress the score on a full set of single-year age dummies and birth-decade dummies, with the 1950s as reference and standard errors clustered on the person. With age fully flexible a decade shift is identified only from the overlap of neighbouring decades at the ages they share, which is the honest version of the mixture's quadratic-in-age-plus-decade specification in Appendix~\ref{app:cohort}.
		\item Subtract each person's decade shift from every one of their scores, and rerun partial K-means with $K=3$ and 50 seeded starts on the adjusted profiles.
		\item Compare the adjusted typology with the unadjusted one person by person, and the two sets of decade shifts with each other.
	\end{itemize}
	Figure~\ref{fig:kmeanscohort} has the result. The decade shifts under flexible age are zero for every cohort born before 1960, within one standard error, and turn negative only from the 1970s on: $-0.11$, $-0.21$, $-0.28$ and $-0.35$ standard deviations of $\theta$ for the 1970s to 2000s. Those cohorts are observed only when young, so a cohort effect and a period effect cannot be told apart there. The mixture's quadratic specification attributes shifts to the earlier decades as well, which is what a quadratic does when the age profile is not quadratic. The typology is unaffected: the adjusted Rand index between the adjusted and unadjusted types is $0.903$ on $\theta$ and $0.956$ on $h$, at least 97\% of people keep their type, and the type shares move by at most half a percentage point.

	\subsection{Types over age windows}
	\label{app:kmeans_windows}

	\begin{figure}[H]\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_windows.png}
		\caption{As Figure~\ref{fig:windows}, for partial K-means: types refitted on sliding twenty-year windows (a) and windows expanding backwards from 90 (b), against the lifecycle typology in grey; (c) the co-assignment correlation with the lifecycle assignment, here the correlation of the two same-type indicators over pairs of people. Lower row: the share of the person-waves observed at each age that each window assigns to its worst type (d, e), and the worst type's share of each window's people (f). The same figure on $h$ is in Appendix~\ref{app:measures}.}
		\label{fig:windows_kmeans}
	\end{figure}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item Partial K-means refitted on the same windows is more stable than the mixture: the sliding windows agree with the lifecycle typology at $0.73$--$0.83$ up to 60--80 and $0.58$ at 70--90, the backward windows at $0.74$ from 60 and $0.95$ from 30. What a late window sees are the types after the divergence phase: the worst type's share of a window's people rises from 15\% (20--40) to 27\% (70--90) as the window moves, and from 18\% to 27\% as the backward window shrinks from 30--90 to 70--90.
		\item The lower row says that this rise is composition, not a growing bad type. Within the ages two windows share, the worst type's share of the person-waves observed at each age is nearly the same whichever window assigned it: the backward windows starting at 30, 40 or 50 sit on the lifecycle typology's line (about 13\% at 45, 18\% at 65, 19\% at 75), so the rise is the window's people getting older. Only the windows confined to late life relabel: a window starting at 60 or 70 puts 21--25\% of the person-waves at 65--75 in its worst type against the lifecycle typology's 18--19\%, picking up people who were middle type for most of their lives and decline late.
		\item Set against Figure~\ref{fig:windows}, the contrast is the point. K-means classifies on person-by-age profiles, so a window's worst type is always the lowest profiles in that window and its share follows the age mix; the mixture classifies on paths with persistence, so its worst class needs the divergence inside the window and can shrink to a small very sick group or grow to a third of people as the window moves. The stability of K-means across windows is its insensitivity to where the decline happens.
	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{The error structure changes the shape of the worst type's path, not its membership.} Of the K-means worst type, 85\% are in the mixture's worst class at $K=3$, 92\% at $K=4$ and 94\% at $K=5$. Yet the two paths differ: the mixture's starts near the average ($+0.35$ at 20) and declines at $0.043$ a year to 50, the K-means type starts a unit lower ($-0.33$) and declines at $0.033$, because the persistent component lets the mixture read a low level at 20 as a carried deviation. The mixture's worst class is also the wider one within (sd $0.81$ against $0.66$ at 50) and its members are held with uncertainty: 45\% of its modal members have a posterior above $0.9$, at every $K$.
		\item \textbf{The number of classes refines who is in the worst type and tightens it, but leaves its mean shape alone.} Under K-means the worst type halves from 18\% to 9\% by $K=5$, keeping 63\% and then 48\% of the $K=3$ members, its within-type sd falls from $0.66$ to $0.55$, and its path keeps the same shape shifted down by a quarter to a third of a unit. Under the mixture the worst class moves from 18\% to 14\% by modal label, keeps 84\% and 76\% of its $K=3$ members, and its path, persistence and within-class spread barely move; the added classes split the middle and the healthy. The two methods converge on the same core: the $K=5$ K-means worst type is almost entirely inside the mixture's worst class.
	\end{itemize}

	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{On the multidimensional classes the gaps are wider, and the ranking of the policies is the same} (Figure~\ref{fig:prevle_md}). The multidimensional worst class is smaller (19\% against 25\%) and more sharply drawn, since the fit's mortality channel pulls the people who go on to die into it, and its hazards are part of the fit rather than estimated afterwards. The LE gap between the best and the worst class from 50 is $12.8$ years against $9.5$ on the univariate classes (from 25, $14.5$ against $10.6$), and the composition gradients the types carry are larger ($2.0$ years by education, $3.0$ by income and $0.9$ across regions from 50, with worst-class membership running from 30\% among people with no qualifications to 12\% among graduates and from 33\% in the bottom income third to 8\% in the top). Treating the worst class at $\mu=1$ from 50 takes the type gap to $8.0$ years (the HLE gap from $30.1$ to $23.5$) and the gradients to $1.1$, $1.8$ and $0.6$; the same budget spread evenly ($\mu=0.17$) again leaves them where they are; population LE rises by $0.8$ years under targeting and $1.0$ under the even spread; and the standard deviation of age at death barely moves ($10.4$ years at baseline, $10.3$ and $10.8$). From 25 the targeted policy takes the worst class level with the best ($62.6$ against $62.2$ years) rather than past it. Here the mortality gradient in $\theta$ is at or below the between-type gradient the fit's own class hazards imply at every age, so the years are if anything conservative, where on the univariate classes the late-life years are if anything generous.
		\item \textbf{On the other measures the type gaps are smaller still, and targeting closes them almost entirely} (Figures~\ref{fig:prevle_h}--\ref{fig:prevle_logfrailty}). The LE gap between the best and the worst class from 50 is $8.5$ years on $h$, $8.3$ on the frailty index and $7.3$ on log frailty, against $9.5$ on univariate $\theta$ and $12.8$ on the multidimensional classes; the gradients the types carry shrink in step ($1.8$, $1.5$ and $1.2$ years by education, $2.3$, $1.9$ and $1.6$ by income). Treating the worst class at $\mu=1$ from 50 takes the type gap to $2.2$, $0.9$ and $0.5$ years and the gradients to within half a year of zero, because on the bounded measures the worst class holds nearly all the preventable decline: the best class sits at the ceiling of $h$ or the floor of frailty with almost nothing to prevent, which is why under uniform prevention at $\mu=1$ on log frailty the middle class ends up ahead of the best ($44.1$ against $38.3$ years). The even spread again leaves the gradients where they are and the standard deviation of age at death moves by at most half a year under any policy. The ranking of policies is therefore the same on every measure; what the measure changes is how much of the decline the worst type owns, as in Figure~\ref{fig:returnsmeasures}. The gradients of mortality and of the healthy share in $h$ and in the frailty index are steep per unit because the scales are bounded, so these versions lean more on the logit links at the ends of the scales.
	\end{itemize}
