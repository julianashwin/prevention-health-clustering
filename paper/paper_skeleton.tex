	\subsection{Setup: deterministic type and stochastic noise}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Health of person $i$ of latent type $j$ at age $a$ (age counted from a base age, not birth):
		\begin{equation}
			h_{i,a} = \bar h_{j,a} + u_{i,a}, \qquad \bar h_{j,a} \equiv H_j + \sum_{a'=1}^{a} d_{j,a'} .
			\label{eq:levels}
		\end{equation}
		$(H_j, d_{j,a})$ is the \emph{deterministic} component: the type's level at the base age and its expected change at each age. $u_{i,a}$ is the \emph{stochastic} component, mean zero within type, otherwise unrestricted (could be transitory, persistent, or measurement error).
		\item Three kinds of information in a health history: 
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item what \emph{stays} (the level, including permanent shocks that have landed),
			\item what is \emph{in the moment} (transitory variation and measurement error), 
			\item how one is \emph{evolving} ($d_{j,a}$, the only component that predicts future \emph{changes}).
		\end{enumerate} 
		Prevention targets the third; treatment acts on the first two as realised.
		\item Only what is carried from one wave to the next produces covariance between waves: the three components leave different traces on the covariance matrix, which is the basis for our identification.
		\item Two-by-two matrix of possibilities: dispersion is deterministic (across types) or stochastic (within type), and actionable or not. Deterministic and actionable is \emph{targetable} prevention; stochastic and actionable is untargeted prevention or treatment. Predictability of levels $\neq$ predictability of changes.
		\item Health types are thus the object of interest: they are the support of $(H, d)$, and clustering estimates it. Between/within variance maps to type/individual. 
		\item Link to other life course: With the base age at birth, the life-course accounts are second moments: critical period $=\Var(H)$, accumulation $=\Var(d)$, pathway $=\Cov(H,d)$; at a later base age the triple's evolution with the base age separates them.
		\item Throughout this section, we will use a linear illustration (constant type slopes, $d_{j,a}=d_j$): a random-coefficients growth model with $V_H, V_d, C_{Hd}$ and noise variances $v_a$,
		\begin{equation}
			\begin{gathered}
				\bar h_a=\bar H+a\bar d, 
				\\ 
				\Var(h_a)=V_H+2aC_{Hd}+a^2V_d+v_a, 
				\\
				\Cov(h_s,h_t)=V_H+(s+t)C_{Hd}+st\,V_d+\Cov(u_s,u_t).
			\end{gathered}
			\label{eq:forward}
		\end{equation}
		\item A key issue that will come up again in relation to costs and when we bring to the data is what the units of health are. The covariance matrix treats health as cardinal, so transformations of a health index that are rank neutral can change the covariance matrix. 
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\subsection{Policy: healthcare cost, treatment and prevention}
	\label{sec:policy_fw}
	
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Cost of poor health $c(h)=(1-h)^2$, full health normalised to one. The quadratic is the second-order approximation to any smooth increasing convex cost and makes the first two moments of health sufficient for every policy object below; Appendix~\ref{app:extras} repeats the returns with the \emph{estimated} cost curve in place of the quadratic. 
		\item \note{On the scale: the covariance channels and the identification results don't rely on a bound. For an unbounded measure like the $\theta$ below, the same algebra goes through with any quadratic; the returns are reported on both scales.}
		\item Population cost at age $a$: mean gap plus between-type plus within-type variance,
		\begin{equation}
			\E[c(h_{i,a})]=(1-\bar h_a)^2+\Var_j(\bar h_{j,a})+\textstyle\sum_j\pi_j\Var(u_{i,a}\mid j).
			\label{eq:popcost}
		\end{equation}
		\item Two instruments. 
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item \textbf{Treatment} is ex post and proportional to the realised gap, from age $r$
			$$
			h\mapsto h+\tau(1-h)
			$$
			\item \textbf{Prevention} is ex ante and scales every future type change by $(1-\mu)$ from age $r$, so health gained is $\mu$ times the type's preventable decline
			$$
			\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}
			$$
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{itemize}
	
	\begin{result}[Return to treatment; note Result 1]\label{res:treat}
		$$
		T(r)=2\sum_{a\ge r}\bigl[(1-\bar h_a)^2+\Var(h_{i,a})\bigr]
		$$
		Twice the expected cost still to come. Cross-sectional moments only; no identification problem.
	\end{result}
	
	\begin{result}[Return to prevention; note Results 2 and 3]\label{res:prev}
		For a uniform policy,
		\begin{equation}
			P(r)=\textstyle\sum_j\pi_jP_j(r)
			=2\sum_{a\ge r}\Bigl[\underbrace{(1-\bar h_a)(\bar h_{r-1}-\bar h_a)}_{\text{mean channel}}
			+\underbrace{\Cov_j\bigl(1-\bar h_{j,a},\,\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}\bigr)}_{\text{between-type channel}}\Bigr],
			\label{eq:P}
		\end{equation}
		The between-type channel splits as $\Cov_j(1-H_j,\,\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})+\Cov_j(H_j-\bar h_{j,r-1},\,\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a}) +\Var_j(\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})$: the pathway, persistence and accumulation margins.
		\\~\\
		By type, $P_j(r)=2\sum_{a\ge r}(1-\bar h_{j,a})(\bar h_{j,r-1}-\bar h_{j,a})$: gap times preventable decline. 
	\end{result}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Treatment reaches all realised dispersion, between and within, but only when gaps are already open. 
		\item Prevention averts future gaps but must be assigned ex ante; the uniform policy pays the population-average preventable decline and its between-type channel is bounded by deterministic heterogeneity. 
		\item Measurement error belongs in neither return and is removed by Case~4 below. 
		\item \note{We should discuss how to treat the ``measurement error" in our policy scenarios later - should they be part of the treatment or not?}
	\end{itemize}
	
	
	\begin{result}[Targeting; note Results 5--7 and Lemma C.1]\label{res:target}
		Any allocation $\mu(z)$ with the same average intensity saves $\Cov(\mu(z),\E[P_i\mid z])$ over the uniform policy, at most $\mathrm{sd}(\mu)\,\mathrm{sd}(\E[P_i\mid z])$: a signal is worth the return dispersion it reveals. Targeting on initial health $h_{i,0}$ collects a gap channel and a compounding channel, both cells of the first row of the covariance matrix, attenuated by the level/noise share $\lambda_0$. The type is the ceiling, $\max_jP_j(r)-\sum_j\pi_jP_j(r)$: returns depend on the person only through the type, so no signal does better, and the gap between what a level collects and the ceiling is what estimating types is worth.
	\end{result}
	
	\paragraph{What each policy object needs (note Result C.1 and Table 2).} In the linear case $\bar h_{j,a}=H_j+a\,d_j$ the between-type channel is $(a-r+1)(C_{Hd}+aV_d)$, so
	\begin{equation}
		P(r)=2\sum_{a\ge r}\bigl[(1-\bar h_a)(\bar h_{r-1}-\bar h_a)\bigr]+2K(r),\qquad
		K(r)=A(r)\,C_{Hd}+B(r)\,V_d,
		\label{eq:Kr}
	\end{equation}
	with known exposure weights $A(r)=\sum_{a\ge r}(a-r+1)$ and $B(r)=\sum_{a\ge r}a(a-r+1)$. This is the precise form of the three statements the structure calls P(i)--P(iii):

	\subsection{Identification}
	\label{sec:ident_fw}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Every observed covariance is a between-type part plus a noise part, $\Cov(h_s,h_t)=\Cov_j(\bar h_{j,s},\bar h_{j,t})+\Cov(u_{i,s},u_{i,t})$. The returns need the first; the data deliver the sum. Assumptions: A1 window linearity ($d_{j,a}=d_j$ over the waves used), A2 orthogonality of the noise to $(H,d)$, A3 a noise class. In the linear case
		\begin{equation}
			\Var(h_a)=V_H+2aC_{Hd}+a^2V_d+v_a,\qquad
			\Cov(h_s,h_t)=V_H+(s+t)C_{Hd}+st\,V_d+\Cov(u_s,u_t).
			\label{eq:forward}
		\end{equation}
	\end{itemize}
	
	\begin{result}[The diagonal alone is not enough; note Lemma A.1]\label{lem:diag}
		With the noise variances $v_a$ unrestricted the variance profile can never rule out pure noise and bounds the deterministic variance only from above. With stationary noise its differences identify $(C_{Hd},V_d)$ but never split $V_H$ from $\sigma^2_u$, which enter every diagonal cell as a bundle. Identification needs a restriction on how the noise variance moves with age and at least one off-diagonal cell.
	\end{result}
	
	\begin{proposition}[Identification within the AR-plus-spike class; note Proposition 1]\label{prop:main}
		Assume A1--A2 and $\Cov(u_s,u_t)=\rho^{\,t-s}v_s+\mathbf 1\{s=t\}\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,s}$ for $s\le t$, $\rho\in[0,1)$: a carried component with memory $\rho$ and variances $v_s$, plus a one-period spike. Then (i) five waves identify $V_H,C_{Hd},V_d,\rho$ and both noise variances at the waves used; (ii) four suffice with the spike absent (Case~3) or with both noise variances stationary (Case~4); (iii) three suffice with the spike absent and the carried variance stationary, whatever $\rho$ (Cases~1--2); (iv) two suffice if in addition $C_{Hd}=0$ and the noise is memoryless.
	\end{proposition}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item The forces, one per region of the matrix. This is the precise content of I(i) and I(ii):
		\begin{itemize}
			\item[I(i)] \textbf{Diagonal} $V(a)$: every component adds variance. Stationary noise cancels in differences and leaves $C_{Hd}$ (slope) and $V_d$ (curvature). Fails when $v_a$ drifts.
			\item[I(ii)] \textbf{First row} $C_0(a)$: the deterministic part is the line $V_H+aC_{Hd}$, carried noise a decay $\rho^av_0$; a line cannot mimic a decay, which splits the level from the noise and measures the memory; the row's slope beyond the noise is $C_{Hd}$ itself, locally $C_{Hd}(s)$ for the row anchored at $s$. A one-period spike is invisible to the row and is read as the anchor's excess over the row's back-extrapolation. Limit: $\rho\to1$ merges permanent shocks with the level.
			\item[I(iii)] \textbf{Interior}: $st\,V_d$ needs both indices to move; delivers $V_d$ when the diagonal cannot be read, overidentifying tests otherwise.
		\end{itemize}
		\item The cases, and where each appears in the paper:
	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item The local reading. A1 holds only over a window, so every read is local. With $C_{Hd}(a)\equiv\Cov_j(\bar h_{j,a},d_j)$, the deterministic variance grows by $C_{Hd}(a)+C_{Hd}(a+1)$ per year: a variance profile that flattens requires $C_{Hd}(a)$ to change sign, which the global linear model cannot produce. 	\subsection{Data and the health measure}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Understanding Society (UKHLS), waves 1--15, 2009--2024 \citep{ukhls}; adults aged 20--90. The SF-12 is asked every wave; the impairment battery every wave (routed by long-standing illness in waves 1--7); the condition inventory at a person's first interview and for new diagnoses since.
		\item \textbf{The measure} (one page; full construction in \code{measuring\_health/health\_measure\_construction.pdf}). A graded-response model \citep{samejima1969} over eight ordinal items: the four SF-12 physical testlets (general health GH, physical functioning PF, role-physical RP, bodily pain BP), three counts over the UKHLS impairment areas (functional: mobility, lifting, dexterity, co-ordination; self-care: personal care, continence; sensory: hearing, sight), and one count of ever-diagnosed physical conditions (0--5, 6+). Sample 387,599 person-waves on 69,958 people, complete on all eight items, 5.5 observations per person.
		\item Reported three ways, all monotone in the same answers: $\theta$ (latent, $N(0,1)$ in the pool), $h$ ($\theta$ through the expected-score curve, on $[0,1]$: the scale the framework's health variable maps to), and a ten-deficit index anyone can compute by hand \citep{mitnitski2001,searle2008}. Spearman $0.991$ between $h$ and the deficit index.
		\item Transparency: the model is the ordinal factor model in another parameterisation \citep{takane1987}, so the score is to a very good approximation a weighted sum of the coded answers ($R^2=0.993$): \textbf{71\% on the SF-12 physical testlets, 23\% on the impairment groups, 6\% on diagnoses}. That is the ``PHYS $\approx\sum_k\lambda_kh_k$'' reading. The deficit index puts 60/30/10 on the same three blocks and reaches the same conclusions.
		\item Why this bank (one sentence each; the 85-candidate search is archived): no floor (7 person-waves at the worst score among 60--84 year olds, against 1.1\% for functioning-only banks), no positive local dependence (max $Q_3=+0.05$), convex in cost on every variant, mental items absent by construction.
	\end{itemize}

	\paragraph{Comparison metrics, mortality and utilisation.}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Against the alternatives the paper does not use: the UKHLS PCS (a 1990s US-normed weighted sum that mixes in the mental items), the MCS, and the raw chronic count (Table~\ref{tab:corr}). The measure is a physical construct: correlation $0.90$--$0.94$ with the PCS, $0.27$ with the MCS.
		\item Mortality (Table~\ref{tab:mort}): the log-odds of dying before the next wave fall by $0.85$ per SD of $\theta$ ($0.66$ on $h$), the death rate in the least healthy tenth is 45 times that in the healthiest, and the slope is close to constant across age bands (slightly steeper with age on $h$).
		\item Utilisation in pounds (the bed-day-weighted cost index of \code{cost\_proxy\_plan.pdf}; Figure~\ref{fig:cost}): cost rises with poor health at an increasing rate on every variant, quadratic $t$ between 10 and 37 with costs capped at the 99th percentile.
		\item Is the mapping consistent with age? (Table~\ref{tab:costage}) The proportional gradient, log points of cost per SD sicker, is hump-shaped in age on every variant, peaking at 45--54. On $\theta$ it runs $0.95$ at 20--34 to $0.89$ at 75--90 and the $z\times$age interaction is $+0.017$ per decade ($t=3.5$): close to age-invariant. On $h$ the fall is a third ($1.23$ to $0.94$, $t=10.2$). Both are right: a standard deviation is a different amount of health on the two scales, and the expected-score curve stretches the unhealthy end where the old sit. The scale-free statement survives on either: at a fixed health decile, cost barely moves with age (sickest decile \pounds2,143 at 20--34 vs \pounds2,136 at 75--90), while mean cost rises 2.6-fold. Ageing raises cost by moving people down a fixed curve.
	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}

		\item \textbf{Variance flattens} (b, e): rises steeply to about 60 and then stops growing, with a dip at 65--70. On $\theta$ it falls after 62. Under stationary noise this says the local covariance $C_{Hd}(a)$ has reached zero by 60 and $\Var(d)$ is falling: interventions on dispersion pay early.
		\item \textbf{Divergence then convergence} (c, f): every row drops sharply in its first year (the one-year spike, health not carried even one year), after which the young rows run flat and the old rows keep falling. 	\subsection{Why and how}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Why the whole lifecycle.} The health-dynamics literature works with the old (HRS, ELSA) or short windows; \citet{denardi2017} splice the PSID early in life with the HRS later, but health is modelled only for the older people; \citet{hosseini2022} build a frailty index on the PSID. The prevention framework says $H_{55}$ is an \emph{outcome} of the dynamics before 55, not an input. And the data say the dynamics differ across the lifecycle (Exhibit~1): starting in late middle age misses the divergence phase.
		\item \textbf{How.} Overlapping cohorts observed over 15 waves: cohort-independent types with cohort-dependent shares. First non-parametric (partial K-means), then parametric (Bayesian mixtures), then against observables.
		\item \textbf{Four challenges} addressed along the way: (1) power and type categorisation (functional forms, stochastic structure, several dimensions); (2) mortality and attrition; (3) cohort effects; (4) units and bounds (ceiling, floor, cardinalisation).
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{(A) Non-parametric types: partial K-means}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Method: each person is a vector over ages 20--90 with most entries missing; distances average squared deviations over observed entries only; $K=3$, 50 seeded starts; people with at least three observed ages (39,004). ``Equal penalty'': every person-wave is punished equally, so the method is neutral about whether the stochastic process is transitory or persistent. \note{Open: the person is assigned to one cluster for life, which does impose that the type is permanent; note the caveat in the text.}
		\item Focus on means because they are what the policy objects use and what the interventions considered shift.
		\item Figure~\ref{fig:clusters}: on $h$ three ordered, non-crossing, fanning paths, shares 10 / 28 / 61\%; the worst type starts below the others at 20 and falls fastest to 60. The deficit index gives the same typology (ARI $0.90$, 97\% of people in the same type). $\theta$ does not: shares 18 / 43 / 40\% and ARI $0.37$ with the $h$ typology, because $\theta$ stretches the healthy end and K-means then splits the healthy majority. \note{This is the units challenge showing up immediately; the paper needs a position on it.}
		\item The stochastic part (middle row): within-type deviations are persistent, with lag-1 autocorrelation $0.62$ in the worst type and $0.42$--$0.44$ elsewhere, decaying to $0.15$--$0.23$ at nine years (the middle type to zero). Persistence is class-graded already in the non-parametric version.
		\item Composition strips: the worst type's share of observed person-waves rises with age on $h$ (7\% at 20 to 13\% at 85). Selection into the observed sample, not people changing type.
		\item \todo{cohort adjustment within K-means: demean across cohorts at the same age before clustering, or cluster on cohort-demeaned profiles; not done.}
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Prevention exercises}
	\label{sec:policy}
	%% =============================================================================
	
	\note{First pass, on the K-means types, all three variants: the framework's returns computed from the smoothed type paths and the population moments of the clustered people, $c(h)=(1-h)^2$, horizon 90, no survival weighting, no discounting. $\theta$ is mapped affinely to $[0,1]$ by its sample range so that the same gap is defined.}
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Prevention versus treatment} (Figure~\ref{fig:returns}, Table~\ref{tab:returns}). $P(r)/T(r)$ falls from about $0.35$ at $r=30$ to $0.20$ at 50 and $0.17$ at 70 on $h$, and the same on $\theta$ and the deficit index. Two thirds of $T$ is the mean gap and one third the variance (mostly within type after 60); $P$ is almost entirely its mean channel once past 40.
		\item \textbf{Timing.} The between-type covariance channel of $P(r)$ is positive for a start at 30 (divergence still to come), and negative from about 45 on, reaching $-40\%$ of $P(50)$ on $h$: starting later, the compression among types works \emph{against} uniform prevention. This is Exhibit~1's sign change priced.
		\item \textbf{Targeting.} At $r=30$ the worst type's return is ten times the best type's on $h$ ($11.8$ vs $1.1$; uniform $3.5$), so type targeting collects $2.4\times$ the uniform return; by 50 the middle type has the highest return and the type gain falls to $0.8\times$, by 70 to $0.8\times$. On $\theta$ the gains are smaller ($1.0\times$ at 30, $0.3\times$ later) because its typology splits the healthy majority rather than isolating the sick tail. \note{The units challenge again: which typology the policy exercise rests on is a substantive choice.}
		\item Skeleton for the full version:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item the same objects on the Bayesian types, with posterior uncertainty, once refitted;
			\item survival-weighted sums $\sum_{a\ge r}S(a\mid r)(\cdot)$ using the class hazards, both ways (the sickest die, prevention raises survival);
			\item targeting on observables versus on early health history versus on the type (the ceiling), priced by the targeting identity of Section~\ref{sec:framework}, using the Section~\ref{sec:observables} partitions;
			\item the additive-prevention robustness check (the multiplicative $(1-\mu)d$ favours decliners by construction), and prevention as moving a person to a better type;
			\item cost sides: $\mu$ and $\tau$ carry no prices, so cross-instrument comparisons are composition-only; state this or attach unit costs from the cost index.
		\end{itemize}
		
		
		\item \textbf{On the AR(1) plus measurement error classes} (Figure~\ref{fig:returnsssm}, Table~\ref{tab:returnsssm}) the picture shifts in the direction the composition strips suggested. The worst class is larger (27\% on $h$) and its path steeper, so the between-type channel of $P(r)$ is \emph{positive} for starts at 30 and 50 on every variant ($+24\%$ of $P(30)$ on $h$, $+30\%$ on $\theta$) and turns negative only from about 60; $P/T$ is higher ($0.44$ at 30 on $h$ against $0.34$ on the K-means types); and type targeting is worth $1.9\times$ the uniform return at 30 on $h$ and $1.1\times$ on $\theta$, declining more slowly with $r$ than on the K-means types. The difference is the classes themselves: once the year-to-year noise is absorbed, the types fan out more and for longer.
	\end{itemize}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item Cost is what $c(h)$ stands for, so its mean and variance by age are the objects $T(r)$ prices directly: the mean is flat to 40 and then rises steeply, from \pounds330 to \pounds960 by 88; its variance rises fourfold. In logs the variance \emph{falls} with age.

	\begin{itemize}
		\item The estimated curve is close to age-invariant: refitted within age bands it lies on top of the pooled curve from 20 to 74, and only the 75--90 band sits above it, by about 10\% at a given health level (\code{paper\_cost\_curve\_by\_band.csv}). 		\item In pounds, treatment from 30 is worth about \pounds31,000 of lifetime cost per unit of $\tau$ on $h$ and uniform prevention \pounds11,000, a ratio of $0.37$ falling to $0.20$ at 70: the same shape as under the quadratic.

That raises the worst type's return (\pounds29,000 at 30 against \pounds5,700 for the best type), raises the type-targeting gain to $1.5\times$ the uniform return at 30 on $h$ ($1.1\times$ on $\theta$), and makes the between-type channel of $P(r)$ negative already at 30 on $h$.
	\end{itemize}
